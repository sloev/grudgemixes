# Provisional mix dossier 01 — The Honour of Vengeance

**Status: DEVELOPMENT DRAFT — not release-ready.**

## Editorial premise

Two early commercial opera excerpts make vengeance an explicit public declaration, but their grievances operate differently. In Saint-Saëns's *Samson et Dalila*, the Philistine High Priest and Dalila conspire to subdue Samson, whom they regard as a threat to their people and religious order. In Verdi's *Rigoletto*, a father and daughter respond to the Duke's abuse and betrayal; the father's plan is calculated, while Gilda's agency and final decision complicate any easy account of shared revenge. The mix should make this contrast audible rather than treating “revenge” as a single motive.

## Draft liner note (approx. 190 words; revise after listening)

Vengeance is rarely a single voice. In Camille Saint-Saëns's *Samson et Dalila*, the Philistine High Priest and Dalila turn political defeat into a private conspiracy: the phrase “Vengeance at Last” names a plan to break Samson by exploiting the intimacy he trusts. Victor recorded the English-language selection in Camden on 25 May 1908, with contralto Louise Homer and baritone Emilio de Gogorza. Thirteen years later, Verdi's *Rigoletto* offers a different register. Evelyn Scotney and Giacomo Rimini perform “Sì, vendetta,” the Act III duet in which Rigoletto's carefully arranged retaliation meets Gilda's troubled participation. One grievance is collective and religious-political; the other is familial, shaped by sexual abuse, betrayal and unequal power. Neither scene is a documentary account, and neither makes revenge morally simple. These records preserve something more revealing: how the operatic stage made retaliation legible as both declaration and strategy. The 1908 Victor issue is securely tied to its matrix and session; the Vocalion recording is identified by catalogue and specialist sessionography, though its exact date and matrix still need confirmation.

## Source and discrepancy notes

- FAU item 57310 lists Victor 87501, Louise Homer and Emilio de Gogorza, single-sided 10-inch 78rpm; matrix/date blank on the item page. UCSB and HRS supply B-6227-2, Camden, 25 May 1908. The 1908 trade ad independently confirms Victor 87501 and English language. One secondary source lists 89501; retain as a discrepancy, but 87501 is supported by the contemporaneous and institutional sources.
- FAU item 54400 lists catalogue 60019, track 2 “Rigoletto - Si vendetta,” and Scotney/Rimini. Mintzer's specialist discography lists session 7176, ca. March 1921, issue 30146 / B60019; the date is tentative and Vocalion matrix data are known to be difficult.
- Full French libretto is used to establish dramatic context, not to claim that the Victor disc contains the French words. The contemporaneous advertisement says the recording is in English.

Keep both tracks in active B status. This is the strongest provisional two-track mix package identified so far, but it is not yet one of the project's “rock-solid” finished mixes. Its remaining work is concrete and bounded: audio/text inspection, item-level transfer documentation, discographic resolution and rights review.

- **Narrative:** Handel's *Alexander's Feast* (1736), using Dryden's 1697 ode adapted by Newburgh Hamilton. Timotheus summons the Furies and the ghosts of Greeks slain in earlier wars; the wider work moves toward Alexander's burning of Persepolis in revenge. Distinguish poetic narrative from historical fact, and do not imply the disc contains the subsequent recitative/Thais scene unless confirmed.
- **Dossier:** candidates/GM-UK-001-handel-revenge-timotheus-1924.md.

### Updated mix logic

The three tracks now create a more coherent historical arc: Handel dramatizes revenge as a musician's incitement of a ruler and collective memory; Saint-Saëns stages a political-religious conspiracy against a resistant leader; Verdi stages familial retaliation after abuse and betrayal. They share an explicit revenge vocabulary but should not be collapsed into one moral category. Handel is the oldest underlying text (1697/1736) but the newest recording (ca. 1924); Saint-Saëns is the earliest securely dated recording (1908); Verdi closes the sequence (ca. 1921). Proposed order: Handel → Saint-Saëns → Verdi, moving from collective historical memory to conspiracy to intimate family revenge. All three remain B and the mix remains a development draft until listening, transcription, transfer documentation and rights review are complete.


## Adjacent mix development: “The Long Grudge” — Japan recording-object check

A potentially valuable Japanese branch now has two distinct kinds of evidence, which must not be conflated. First, 78MUSIC lists Yoshida Naramaru's *Akō Gishiden: Denchū Ninjō* as Nipponophone/Royal 1651 under the Royal red-label eagle section; if that label classification applies to the surviving disc, Royal's reported discontinuation in 1915 would put the issue safely before the core cutoff. This remains conditional secondary discography evidence, not an inspected label or dated advertisement. Second, Tokyo's Edo-Tokyo Museum independently catalogues a Royal-labelled SP set titled *Akō Gishiden*, collection ID 98651727–98651728, but dates it only to the 20th century and does not identify the performer or matrix. It is a separate C lead, not proof of 1651's identity or chronology.

The narrative cycle is promising for a mix about delayed, collective revenge, but “Akō Gishiden” is a repertory umbrella, not a transcript. Each episode must be checked for what is actually narrated: the insult and sentence, the retainers' vow, the raid, and the aftermath are different dramatic moments. A farewell or biographical episode should not be included merely because it belongs to the same cycle. No Japanese recording is yet promoted to mix-track status: exact audio, performed text, date, transfer provenance and rights remain open.
