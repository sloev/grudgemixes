# Egypt: Dinshaway mawwal / ballad tradition (1906)

Date: 2026-11-15  
Researcher: ChatGPT  
Status: **TEXTUAL / ORAL-TRADITION LEAD — no eligible recording identified**

## Why this lead matters

Ziad Fahmy's study *Ordinary Egyptians: Creating the Modern Nation through Popular Culture* describes the near-immediate commemoration of the 1906 Dinshaway incident in rural colloquial Egyptian *mawāwīl* (singular *mawwāl*, ballad-like sung narratives). The book reports that these songs circulated widely in the countryside and that many were probably not composed by a single identifiable writer. One mawwāl is attributed to Sheikh Mustafa ʿAjāj.

Fahmy summarizes the ʿAjāj ballad as construing the incident as a gendered honour violation: British soldiers injured an Egyptian woman in her father's barn, and revenge is framed as the means to restore the violated honour. The text names Lord Cromer and concludes with the phrase “ghabi ingilizi yastahil al-darb bil-naʿl” (approximately, “a stupid Englishman who deserves to be beaten with a shoe”). Fahmy cites Safwat Kamal, *Al-Ghinaʾ al-Shaʿbi al-Misri: Mawawil wa Qisas Ghinaʾiyya Shaʿbiyya* (1994), pp. 92–94, for an Arabic transcript.

This is a strong thematic lead because the song's reported logic is injury → collective/national honour → retaliatory punishment. It is not merely a song containing a threat. But the source's description is a scholar's summary, not yet a full transcription and translation of the mawwāl. It should not be flattened into a generic “anti-colonial revenge song”: the reported version is specifically gendered, vernacular and rooted in a particular local incident.

## Historical and cultural context

The Dinshaway incident occurred in 1906 under British occupation. The subsequent trial and punishments became a major focus of Egyptian nationalist mobilization and mass-media commemoration. Fahmy discusses the event across the press, poetry, theatre and rural oral culture. He notes that a stage play, *Hadithit Dinshaway* (“The Dinshaway Incident”), was being planned shortly after the court ruling but was quickly banned by the Egyptian interior ministry.

The oral ballad must be kept distinct from Hafiz Ibrahim's contemporary fuṣḥā poem and other literary memorial poems; the planned and banned stage play; later nationalist retellings; and any specific phonograph recording, which has not yet been located.

The oral tradition's attribution to Mustafa ʿAjāj is not enough to establish authorship or a death date. Fahmy says many of the mawāwīl probably did not have a single author. If the item is traditional and its oral provenance can be established, it may meet the composition-side rule; this remains to be assessed from the actual text and transmission history.

## Recording investigation

No pre-1926 shellac or wax-cylinder recording of the Mustafa ʿAjāj Dinshaway mawwāl has been identified in this pass. Searches used English and transliterated forms including “Mawwal Dinshaway,” “Mustafa Ajaj,” “Hadithit Dinshaway,” and Arabic forms for the incident and gramophone recording. Results confirm the ballad's existence as a reported oral song, but not a commercial or archival sound recording.

This negative result is provisional. Early Egyptian commercial recording was already substantial by 1903–1906:
- Gramophone/Zonophone recording campaigns began in Egypt in 1903, with further campaigns in Alexandria, Cairo and Bilād al-Shām.
- Odeon and Baidaphon entered the market in the mid-1900s.
- Contemporary Egyptian recording catalogues and record holdings cover a large vernacular repertoire, but surviving online discographies do not necessarily index every title or oral genre.
- The Gramophone Company's 1903 Egypt discography and the broader Kelly database are useful matrix-based discovery routes; local Egyptian library catalogues and the 1998 *Fihris al-Musiqa wa al-Ghinaʾ al-ʿArabi al-Qadim: al-Musajala ʿAla Istiwanat* are further routes.

Do not treat a search-engine failure as proof that no recording exists. Equally, do not promote this to an eligible track on the basis of a plausible recording environment.

## Taxonomy

**Provisional classification: collective retaliation / honour-restoration rhetoric, with a gendered trigger.**

The reported ballad appears to imagine retaliatory humiliation or punishment of the colonial authority as a response to an injury understood as dishonouring. It may be closer to retaliatory justice or collective vengeance than to a private, calculated grudge. The exact wording and narrative sequence must be checked against Kamal's Arabic transcript before fixing the taxonomy.

## Control example: topical Egyptian mawwāl recorded in 1908 (2026-11-15)

AMAR Foundation documents several 1908 commercial recordings commemorating Mustafa Kamil, including mawwāl recordings with catalogue and matrix details. One example is a mawwāl recorded by Gramophone in 1908 as catalogue 012250, matrix 37P, with takht accompaniment. This is **not** the Dinshaway mawwāl attributed to Sheikh Mustafa ʿAjāj and must not be treated as its identification. Its value is narrower: it demonstrates that topical, locally performed Egyptian mawwāl entered commercial recording circulation within two years of the 1906 incident. That makes a catalogue search historically plausible, not proven.

Source: AMAR Foundation, “007 – Muṣṭafa Kāmil Bāshā – Min Al Tarikh”: https://www.amar-foundation.org/007-mustafa-kamel/ .

**Decision unchanged:** the Dinshaway mawwāl remains an oral/textual lead only. No exact recording, master, label, matrix, raw transfer or rights evidence has been identified.


### Identification refinement for the 1908 control disc

The Arabic AMAR page identifies the single-sided 012250 / matrix 37P item as **“Yā Mufrad al-Ghīd”**, sung by ʿAbd al-Ḥayy Ḥilmī, and says it appears to have been recorded around Mustafa Kamil’s death, with a prayer for mercy for him. Keep this distinct from Ibrahim al-Qabbani’s separately documented two-sided “Mustafa Kamil” mawwāl (Gramophone G.C.11-12282 / G.C.11-12283; matrices 11948b / 11949b). The 012250 item is therefore a topical contemporary mawwāl reference, but the title itself is not “Mustafa Kamil.”

## Variant lead: “Mawwal Zahran” / “Mawwal Shanq Zahran” (2026-11-15)

A further search surfaced a second named Dinshaway ballad tradition, commonly referred to as **Mawwal Zahran** or **Mawwal Shanq Zahran** (“Zahran’s hanging”). This should be tracked alongside, not automatically equated with, the Sheikh Mustafa ʿAjāj mawwāl discussed above.

Evidence and cautions:
- A 2015 *Al-Ahram* cultural-history article says Umm Kulthum sang “Mawwal Shanq Zahran” as a young girl. This is a retrospective newspaper claim, not an identified recording, contemporary programme, dated performance notice or source audio. It does not establish that she recorded it commercially.
- A 2021 article in *Journal of Sciences and Arts of Music* (Helwan University, vol. 46, special issue, August 2021), in a discussion of Sheikh Imam’s national-song repertoire, reproduces a short passage attributed to “Mawwal Shanq Zahran”: “من بعد حكم المحاكم والشاويش والانجليز اتفرعنوا / بعد ما كانوا أوباش نزلوا على دنشواي ما خلو نفر ولا أخوه” (roughly: after the courts’ ruling, the constables and Englishmen grew tyrannical; once they were ruffians, they descended on Dinshaway and spared no one). The article's note points to a 1998 publication by Muhammad al-Shafi, Egyptian General Authority for Cultural Palaces, but the bibliography entry is incomplete. This is useful text evidence, but it does not identify the age or author of the ballad or a recording.
- A 2015 *Al-Ahram* account independently says Umm Kulthum sang the Zahran mawwāl in childhood, but does not supply a date, lyrics, venue or recording medium. A Sama3y forum post repeats a claim that no recording exists and that she sang it in village performances; treat this as collector discussion, not archival proof.
- A separate 2016 *Watani* article attributes to Umm Kulthum a late-1960s recollection to writer Saad al-Din Wahba about the ballad. This is mediated recollection, not a transcript or sound document. It may point to an unpublished film research file or interview notes.

The taxonomy appears to involve **communal grievance and commemorative protest**, with the execution of Zahran as its central image. The surviving quoted lines focus on state/court and colonial violence; they do not by themselves prove a revenge narrative. Do not label it revenge without the complete text and context. Keep the Sheikh Mustafa ʿAjāj ballad’s gendered honour/revenge framing separate until a textual comparison establishes whether these are variants, separate songs, or overlapping oral repertories.
