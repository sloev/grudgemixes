# Grudge Mixes × Insane Music: the series, the tracks and what they say

**Status: EDITORIAL PROPOSAL, 2026-11-20. Nothing here is selected for release, listened to in full, or cleared.** This file is the repository copy of the shared planning doc (https://claude.ai/artifact/WWno4mRY6qUSv3dyz7JQpn). The companion clearance list for the publisher is `research/series-clearance-tracklist-2026-11-20.md`. Each project keeps its own admission rules: Grudge Mixes follows `METHODOLOGY.md`; Insane Music follows `projects/insane-music/method.md`. Placing an item on a volume does not change its evidence grade.

## The angle

A song can be a grievance put on the record by someone who had no court, newspaper or committee to file it with. That is the idea that joins the two projects. The book treats keeping a grudge as a serious practice with archives, committees and heirs. The mixes are the sound of that archive.

**Grudge Mixes** is the formal register: early commercial and field recordings, before 1926, where an injury is named, an offender accused, a vow made or a debt carried down the generations. It shows that grudge-keeping is ordinary, old and everywhere: an Armenian march still owed for a defeat in 451, a Japanese storyteller's version of the 47 retainers, a Mexican corrido naming a traitor, an Appalachian ballad calling Robert Ford a coward.

**Insane Music** (working title *Music in Spite*) is the register of people whose complaints were least likely to be heard: patients, prisoners, detainees, Hansen's-disease residents, people at home with a four-track and no label. Many recorded in spite of the place they were in. Here the other party is often the institution itself.

What we try to convey:

- **Resentment has a history and a craft.** The music proves the book's point: people organised, preserved and passed on their grievances long before anyone studied it.
- **Who gets to keep a grudge in public has never been equal.** A Victor Red Seal tenor could sing revenge for a living. A patient on a hospital ward, or a detainee on Leros smuggling out a cassette, kept theirs at real risk. Putting them side by side is the argument.
- **Spite is not only small.** On spitebites.party spite is a joke about Secret Santa. In these recordings it is also refusal, survival and a record kept against forgetting. The joke works because the archive is real.
- **No moral, no mockery.** Like the book, the mixes do not tell anyone to forgive, and they never make fun of the people singing. The humour stays aimed at the grudge, never at the singer.
- **The whole world, told precisely.** Local words stay in the original language (*kataki-uchi*, *corrido*, *fedayi* song), and no culture stands in for a whole country.

## How the two projects meet

They share a series, not a rulebook. Each volume says which project it belongs to, and each project keeps its own admission test.

| | Grudge Mixes | Insane Music |
| --- | --- | --- |
| What qualifies | A recorded grievance-response: revenge, retaliation, punishment, accusation, curse, feud, defiance, inherited grievance | An extant recording by people outside dominant music institutions: patients, prisoners, detainees, residents of closed settlements, self-taught and DIY makers |
| Date rule | Recorded before 1926 | Any date; obscurity is a selection criterion |
| Typical source | 78s and cylinders in LOC, UCSB/DAHR, FAU, national archives | Hospital tapes, prison reels, clandestine cassettes, community CDs, collectors' small-web archives |
| The other party | A rival, a traitor, an occupier, a family | Often the institution, the state or simply being unheard |
| Main risk | Treating a title as proof of motive | Exploiting suffering, exposing people without consent |

Where they overlap, a track can sit on both shelves: early recordings made in confinement (the 1915–18 prisoner-of-war recordings) and grievance songs made by the marginalised (a woman's 1925 warning against marriage, Armenian immigrant brothers in 1910 New York singing of armed return).

Tracks are chosen by three tests: the song itself carries a grievance or a refusal (not just a sad or angry mood); the exact recording can be identified; and the volume as a whole covers more than one region, language and kind of maker.

## The tracklists

Each volume lists its tracks in running order, with the exact recording and one line on what the track tells. Grades and rights notes are in the clearance list.

| # | Track | Recording | What it tells |

| 1 | Torcom Bézazian, "Bam Porotan" (GM-AM-001) | Victor 69102-A, New York, 1916 | Alishan's 1850 poem calls later Armenians to answer the defeat of Vardan at Avarayr in 451. Fourteen centuries of debt, sung as a march. |
| 2 | Tashjian Brothers, "Menk Bedk E Grvenk" (GM-AM-005) | Columbia E661, New York, 1910 | Three immigrant brothers record a *fedayi* song of armed return. Its refrain was still on posters in 1990. |

**What the mix tells:** a grudge outlives its originators when it is given a form: a poem, a story cycle, a family duty. It is the sound of Part VI.

## Gaps, ethics and clearance

The biggest risk is not copyright but context. The *Music in Spite* volumes should never be sold as a joke product.

- **Keep the shelves apart in tone.** spitebites.party sells chocolate for people you dislike. Vols. 1–4 can share that wink. Vols. 5–7 should stay under *Music in Spite*, with liner notes in the book's sober voice and no spitebites merch tied to them.
- **Consent and community.** Kalaupapa, Ōshima, Lautarchiv POWs and the Enevig travellers were recorded by institutions or researchers, sometimes under coercion. Ask the descendant community or the holding archive before release, and offer a share of proceeds or a free copy. The Lautarchiv calls its POW holdings a sensitive collection.
- **Living and recent people.** Tōjō died in Sep 2026; Indian, Korean and Brazilian makers are mostly alive. Their releases need the makers' or their families' permission, not just an archive's.
- **Held back on purpose.** "Little Mary Phagan" (it accuses Leo Frank, lynched in 1915) and the Drapchi nuns' cassette (the nuns had their sentences extended for recording it) stay off the series.
- **Copyright pattern.** Early recordings published by 1925 are now out of protection in the US, UK and EU. What needs clearing is mostly compositions and translations by people who died after 1955 (Ray Lopez d.1979, Thomas A. Dorsey d.1993), unpublished field recordings, every post-1960s Insane Music track, and the terms of each archive transfer. Not legal advice.
- **Still missing.** No Arabic, Persian, Chinese or Indigenous-Australian track is on a volume yet. Insane Music's psychiatric core in Africa and the Middle East is empty, and everything in Vols. 5–7 still awaits archive requests and listening.
