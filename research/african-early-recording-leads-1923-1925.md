# African early-recording leads: South Africa 1923 and Anlo-Ewe 1925

**Date:** 2026-11-15  
**Status:** Screening note; neither item is yet commercially cleared.  
**Scope:** Two commercial Zonophone leads from the Gramophone Company's London recording activity, kept distinct from ethnographic field recordings.

## 1. Sol T. Plaatje — “A Band of Hard-Pressed Men Are We” (Zonophone 4169, 1923)

**Status: PARTIALLY VERIFIED / STRONG TEXTUAL LEAD / AUDIO PROVENANCE AND RIGHTS UNRESOLVED**

### Discographic identity

- Performer: Solomon Tshekisho (Sol) Plaatje (1876–1932), vocal.
- Language: isiXhosa (historical sources often spell “Si-Xosa”).
- Title: “A Band of Hard-Pressed Men Are We”; Flat International discography gives the Xhosa-language performance title in English translation.
- Label/catalogue: Zonophone 4169; side number X-7-32087.
- Matrix/take: Bb3641-3.
- Recording session: Hayes, Middlesex, UK; 23 October 1923 (the earlier 16 October session was retaken; the third take was issued).
- Piano: Mr. Grant and Madame Adami are identified in Siemon Allen's discographical reconstruction for the 23 October retake; the original 16 October Plaatje session used Sylvia Colenso.
- Coupled side: “The Kaffir Wedding Song” (John Knox Bokwe), matrix Bb3642-3, Zonophone 4169 / X-7-32088.
- Plaatje was in Britain as a member and former general secretary of the South African Native National Congress (SANNC, later ANC); he had previously travelled to protest the 1913 Natives Land Act. This is relevant biography, but does **not** prove that this hymn is a protest song about the Act.

### Text and authorship: important correction to the apparent political reading

The English hymn text is documented in *The Fellowship Hymn Book* (1908), no.19, as **“A Band of Hard-Pressed Men”**, by **Edwin Gilbert**, set to **“ALSACE”**, attributed to Beethoven. The identity/dates of the hymn author need a small qualification: Hymnary's text page cites **Edwin Gilbert, 1859-**, its linked authority record gives **1848–1926**, and another Hymnary search result gives **1859–c.1935**. The birth-year discrepancy should be retained pending authoritative biographical resolution; all of these records place the author's death before the project's 1956 cutoff. Beethoven died in 1827. The text itself is securely published in 1908.

The original English text makes the thematic content more precise than the title alone suggests. It speaks of a hard-pressed collective, hardship, “bitterness of heart,” “hate and want of charity,” daily bread and fear; but the prayer asks for the better part, charity, love and strength to face life's strife. It is a Christian hymn of shared hardship and moral self-examination, not a call to retaliate against an identified adversary. The recorded isiXhosa text appears to be a translation/adaptation of this hymn. Plaatje's translation work is documented in the same period: Siemon Allen notes that Plaatje had returned to Britain partly to seek funding to publish his translation of the Fellowship Hymn Book used by the International Brotherhood Congress. The precise translator credit for this particular Xhosa version should still be checked against the manuscript/hymnbook.

**Provisional taxonomy:** collective hardship / acknowledged bitterness and resentment / religious consolation. It is relevant to a broad grudge-and-resentment anthology as a counterpoint about the naming and disciplining of bitterness, but it is not spite, retaliation or revenge on current evidence. Do not frame it as a coded anti-Land Act song without lyric or contextual evidence.

### Audio, archival source and rights

- Wits University Historical Papers Research Archive lists the Plaatje recording collection (ZA HPRA A1742) and a digitised item A1742-1 containing the songs; the item is catalogued under **16 October 1923**. This date must not be assigned automatically to every track: Flat International's matrix-based reconstruction says the two later sides were retaken on **23 October 1923**, and identifies the issued Bb3641-3 take of “A Band of Hard-Pressed Men Are We” as that later session. The Wits item may group material from both sessions, but this has not been proven. Its collection description also records a physical damaged Zonophone 4168 disc in the Plaatje papers; the exact original source and take of the digitised audio need confirmation.
- Flat International's South African Audio Archive reproduces label images and discographic reconstruction, and identifies the 4169 matrices/takes. It is a research/discography source, not itself proof that an accessible digital file is an unedited archival transfer.
- A reissue or tape copy must not be treated as the required raw archival transfer of the original shellac. Obtain Wits/other archive item-level transfer notes and the original 4169 disc provenance.
- Rights to the 1908 hymn text and Beethoven tune may be public domain, but the 1923 sound recording, performance, translation, and archival digital transfer must be assessed separately by territory and intended use. **No commercial clearance established.**

## 2. Roland C. Nathaniels — “Ewe Battle Hymn” (Zonophone 3972, 1925)

**Status: HIGH-PRIORITY LEAD / NOT YET A CANDIDATE / NOT CLEARED**

### Discographic identity

- Performer: Roland C. Nathaniels, Anlo-Ewe musician from Keta, described by Ernest Owusu-Poku as a trained composer, pianist and organist.
- Language: Ewe.
- Title: “Ewe Battle Hymn.”
- Label/catalogue: Zonophone 3972 (10-inch, 78rpm shellac).
- Side number: X-4-42857.
- Matrix/take: Yy7339-2.
- Recording date: 20 November 1925, in the London-based Gramophone Company/Zonophone West African recording campaign.
- Discography describes the item as **“Native melody”** (unaccompanied in the catalogue entry). That is a useful provenance clue but is not, by itself, sufficient proof that the composition is traditional or that the recording reproduces an established Anlo war-song text.
- The coupled side is “Anlo Patriotic Song,” X-4-42856 / matrix Yy7340-1, recorded the same day; it is described as Ewe, song with piano and whistling chorus.

### Historical and genre context

Owusu-Poku's study documents Nathaniels's 1925 recording contract and lists Zonophone 3972 as “Anlo patriotic song & Ewe battle hymn.” The same study says the Anlo Patriotic Song was later published as sheet music by Eclipse Music Publishing Company in Britain on 1 January 1926. That publication date is after the project's cutoff, although the master was fixed on 20 November 1925.

Research on Anlo-Ewe musical traditions describes war songs as repertoire sung by warriors before, during and after battle: they can encourage courage, dare opponents, expose an enemy's weaknesses, recount victories and preserve military memory. Some Anlo war-song genres are tied to particular dance-drumming practices (including Kpegisu and Atrikpi). This is relevant cultural context, but no source yet identifies the 1925 “Ewe Battle Hymn” as a particular Kpegisu/Atrikpi song, nor connects its lyrics to a named historical battle or revenge episode.

**Provisional taxonomy:** battle / collective resistance / martial memory. Whether it contains revenge, retaliation, grievance or merely martial exhortation is unknown until the Ewe text is recovered and translated with competent language assistance. Do not equate “battle hymn” with revenge.

### Authorship, audio and rights

- The discography's “Native melody” description suggests a traditional melody, but composition/lyric provenance and the exact relation of Nathaniels's arrangement to any older song remain open.
- Nathaniels's own dates and the authorship of any lyrics are not established in the sources consulted. The recording's 1925 fixation date meets the recording-date cutoff; the composition/traditional-provenance gate is not yet proven.
- The 1925 disc is a commercial shellac issue, not a field recording. No original copy, item-level archive transfer, direct audio, or transfer notes have yet been identified. Later compilations/reissues are discovery/listening leads only and do not establish an eligible raw transfer.
- Commercial rights to composition, performance, master and archival transfer remain unresolved.

### Separate related title: “Anlo Patriotic Song”

Zonophone 3972's other side, “Anlo Patriotic Song,” has matrix Yy7340-1 and was also recorded on 20 November 1925. Owusu-Poku reports that it was later issued as sheet music on 1 January 1926. Do not merge it with “Ewe Battle Hymn”: it is a distinct song, described as a piano-accompanied song with whistling chorus, and its authorship/lyrics and cutoff eligibility require separate checks. It may be a relevant collective-political expression, but no revenge mechanism has been established.

## Linguistic qualification — “Orin Ofo” is not yet securely translated as “song of loss”

The earlier screening note treated *Ofo* as a likely Yoruba word for loss/calamity and used Afrodisc's “Lament” genre label to classify the side as a grief/loss lead. That remains plausible, but the unmarked catalogue title is **lexically ambiguous** and should not be presented as a settled translation.

- Samuel Crowther's 1852 *A Vocabulary of the Yoruba Language* lists **Ofo** with senses including “emptiness” and “loss, calamity, damage”; its historical orthography does not supply the modern tone-marked distinction needed here.
- Modern Yoruba sources distinguish **òfò** (“loss,” including loss by death in some glossaries) from **ọ̀fọ̀** (“incantation,” a verbal/ritual genre). Scholarship treats ọ̀fọ̀ as a specific Yoruba incantatory form, which can be used for different purposes. This is not synonymous with a lament.
- Afrodisc's specialist discography labels the Zonophone side **“Lament”**. That is evidence for the cataloguer's genre identification, but it does not provide the original text or explain the classification.
- A 2013 study of Yoruba folk musical idioms distinguishes *Orin Ìdárò* (dirge) from other song categories; a 2020 classification lists *ofo* among incantatory forms. These sources underline why the missing tones and text matter.

**Corrected status: HIGH-PRIORITY TEXTUAL LEAD / NOT YET TRANSLATED / NOT A CANDIDATE.** Record the catalogue form exactly as **ORIN OFO**; do not silently add tone marks or translate it as “song of loss.” Retain “Lament” as the Afrodisc catalogue description, not an independently verified translation. Possible readings include a lament associated with loss or a song involving *ọ̀fọ̀* incantation; no claim about content or ritual function can be made until the recording is heard and the Yoruba words are transcribed and reviewed by a competent speaker/scholar.

## New Anlo-Ewe research branch: *haló* song-poetry, retaliation and the 1925 Nathaniels disc

### *Haló*: an exceptionally strong thematic tradition, attested from ca.1912

Daniel K. Avorgbedor's peer-reviewed article, “Freedom to Sing, License to Insult: The Influence of *Haló* Performance on Social Violence Among the Anlo Ewe,” *Oral Tradition* 9/1 (1994), pp.83–112, dates this musical-dramatic genre from approximately **1912** until its official proscription in 1960. It combines insult songs, dance, drumming, mime, poetry, spoken forms and visual symbols. Rival villages or wards exchange provocations, sung and spoken insults, accusations and counterattacks. Triggers include inter-ward marriage/wife-taking, insults about another group's music, personal insults and existing hostilities. Performances could escalate into physical confrontation and persist for months or years.

Most important for this compendium, Avorgbedor explicitly describes an injured party seeking **vindication and revenge through song** when no formal channel can condemn an inter-ward marriage. Group-backed songs communicate grievances and aggressive intent; counterattacks follow. Wife-taking is described as a direct aggravation to an individual and ward, with song-counterattack as the immediate response. This is a very strong fit for grievance, retaliation and revenge-through-song, not merely generic martial music.

**Date qualification:** the genre is attested from ca.1912 and thus existed within the core period. This does not date any particular song text or recording. Avorgbedor's retrospective fieldwork does not establish that a specific *haló* song was commercially recorded on a 78. Treat this as a high-priority repertoire trail, not an eligible recording yet. The tradition's own conventions included “burying” songs after a winner was determined; the texts could be highly individualized and sensitive. That may help explain why surviving texts are difficult to locate.

Primary source: Daniel K. Avorgbedor, “Freedom to Sing, License to Insult: The Influence of *Haló* Performance on Social Violence Among the Anlo Ewe,” *Oral Tradition* 9/1 (1994): 83–112, https://journal.oraltradition.org/wp-content/uploads/files/articles/9i/4_avorgbedor.pdf (especially pp.84–88, 92–95).

### Yakagbe and Kpegisu: a separate revenge narrative embedded in war-song history

Justice Stephen Kofi Gbolonyo's University of Pittsburgh thesis, *Want the History? Listen to the Music! Historical Evidence in Anlo Ewe Musical Practices: A Case Study of Traditional Song Texts* (MA thesis, 2005), describes Gadzo, Atrikpui, Atsiagbeko and Kpegisu as Anlo-Ewe war dance/drumming genres, with origins attributed by cited scholarship to 1650–1886. Their songs could be performed before, during and after war, including mourning defeat and celebrating victory; texts use older Ewe and sometimes Fon or other linguistic layers, with metaphor and allusion.

A striking account concerns **Yakagbe**, whose only son was killed in one of Anlo's wars. Gbolonyo says she vowed revenge; her lament criticized the inadequate preparation for the battle, and she defied gender convention by joining the men at the front to retaliate. Her victory and later military deeds are given as the origin story for Kpegisu, a war dance instituted in honour of brave women. This is an explicit injury → lament/accusation → vow of revenge → direct retaliation narrative within Anlo song-and-dance history.

Caution: this is a 2005 scholarly account of oral history, not a contemporary 18th/19th-century document. The thesis does not identify Yakagbe's lament as a specific pre-1926 recording or reproduce its full text. It is a textual/repertoire lead, not evidence that the 1925 Zonophone “Ewe Battle Hymn” contains the Yakagbe story.

Gbolonyo also transcribes *Koku: “Kom Da de Gbe Adewo Dzi”*, a song about a white man/European who inflicted pain, distorted the speaker's life and insulted ancestors, and *Adzohu Kadodo: “Atsom Do Gbexo Mi Yia”*, which describes strangers treating the people as inferior and an impending clash. These are grievance/resistance texts, but the thesis's oral-history attribution is not a pre-1926 date for any recorded version.

Source: Justice Stephen Kofi Gbolonyo, *Want the History? Listen to the Music! Historical Evidence in Anlo Ewe Musical Practices* (University of Pittsburgh MA thesis, 2005), https://d-scholarship.pitt.edu/7097/1/JSKGbolonyo_etdPitt2005%5B1%5D.pdf (especially pp.61, 68–73, 94–98).

### A second Anlo-Ewe grievance genre: *haló* is not the same as war-song

*Haló* is a competitive song-poetry confrontation, not a military war-dance genre. Avorgbedor says compositions draw on negative private histories, personal biographies and sometimes fabricated constructions; groups compose fresh songs in response to previous attacks. A separate 2002 student study by Corinna Campbell reports the tradition's local sensitivity and cites colonial-government documents banning *haló* performances by 1918, while some informants traced it further back. Treat that earlier dating as a lead; Avorgbedor (1994) is the stronger source for ca.1912–1960. Campbell's introductory fieldwork paper: https://digitalcollections.sit.edu/african_diaspora_isp/44 .

## 2026-11-15 — Anlo-Ewe oral-history text cross-check (Gbolonyo 2005)

A closer reading of Justice Stephen Kofi Gbolonyo's University of Pittsburgh MA thesis, *Want the History? Listen to the Music! Historical Evidence in Anlo Ewe Musical Practices: A Case Study of Traditional Song Texts* (2005), supplies a more specific Anlo-Ewe repertoire context than the broad “war-song tradition” summary above. It does **not**, however, identify the text of Nathaniels's 1925 “Ewe Battle Hymn.”

### Texts and themes documented independently of Zonophone 3972

- In a discussion of Anlo political and military song (thesis pp.52–53), Gbolonyo describes a song warning attackers that harm done to Anlo will not be forgotten merely because no immediate response follows: the community will respond in due course. This is a direct collective grievance / delayed-retaliation theme. The excerpt available in the thesis does not securely give the song's title or full Ewe text in the same passage; do not invent one or attach it to Nathaniels.
- The thesis transcribes an *Atamga/Atrikpui* song, “Atamga Ya De Mie Gbloe Dzro,” with a literal translation about a sworn oath that may have to be broken because “so long as the enemy persists” the community cannot stand aside. Gbolonyo interprets it as defensive resistance to an ongoing attack, not private revenge (pp.54–55).
- An *Akpoka* song, “Mila yi Ahoe,” includes the line rendered “When the day of reckoning is come, / You would hear from us.” Gbolonyo interprets it in a historical narrative of escape from slave traders / oppression, while noting competing accounts linking it to Agokoli/Notsie or European encounter and slavery. Its precise origin is uncertain; the text expresses deferred response and hope for freedom (pp.74–75).
- Gbolonyo also recounts Yakagbe, an Anlo woman whose son was killed in war: she vowed revenge, joined the male fighters, and her courage is associated with the later institution of *kpegisu* in her honour (pp.95–96). This is a strong revenge narrative in Anlo cultural memory, but the thesis does not establish that any particular Yakagbe song is the 1925 recording.

### Editorial consequence for “Ewe Battle Hymn”

This research upgrades the **contextual plausibility** that an Anlo-Ewe martial song could articulate collective injury, delayed retaliation or resistance. It does not upgrade the recording's thematic evidence: “Native melody” and “Ewe Battle Hymn” remain catalogue descriptors, not a transcript. The 1925 recording stays **C — LEAD** until the actual Yy7339-2 performance is heard and transcribed, and its relationship (if any) to *Atamga*, *Atrikpui*, *Akpoka*, *Kpegisu* or another named tradition is established by evidence.

## Haló: historically documented grievance-performance tradition (context, not yet a recording match)

A stronger thematic research path has emerged around Anlo-Ewe *haló* (also written “halo” in some catalogues and student work). Daniel K. Avorgbedor describes it as a multi-media sociomusical drama involving insult songs, dance, drumming, mime, poetry, spoken forms, costume and visual devices. He dates its popularity from approximately 1912 until its official proscription in 1960. Performances generally involved rival wards or villages and combined provocation, accusation, sung/spoken insults and competitive musical display; the exchanges could escalate into physical violence and persist beyond the performance. Avorgbedor explicitly describes song as a means of seeking “vindication and revenge” where an aggrieved person or ward lacked an effective official remedy, and describes counterattack through song as a common response to wife-taking and other affronts. This is unusually close to the compendium’s broadened grievance/retaliation scope. Source: Daniel K. Avorgbedor, “Freedom to Sing, License to Insult: The Influence of *Haló* Performance on Social Violence Among the Anlo Ewe,” *Oral Tradition* 9.1 (1994), 83–112, especially pp. 84–89, https://journal.oraltradition.org/wp-content/uploads/files/articles/9i/4_avorgbedor.pdf .

### Dating and evidentiary cautions

- Avorgbedor’s circa-1912 start date is a retrospective ethnomusicological statement, not itself a located 1912 primary document. His footnote ties the 1960 prohibition to Criminal Code Act 29, section 295; it does not identify a 1912 ban or first performance record.
- Corinna Campbell’s 2002 SIT fieldwork paper says colonial-government documents concerning bans on the *halóga* (announcement of a haló performance) indicate the songs were established by 1918. The accessible repository record confirms the paper and its abstract, but the underlying archival document and its full citation have not yet been independently inspected. Treat “by 1918” as a promising secondary lead, not verified archival fact. Repository record: https://digitalcollections.sit.edu/african_diaspora_isp/44 .
- Avorgbedor’s examples include a “pre-haló” song and a 1957 haló; the latter is explicitly post-cutoff. The contemporary songs printed in the article are not evidence of pre-1926 texts and must not be backdated.
- *Haló* is a social performance system, not simply a synonym for any Ewe insult song or “battle hymn.” No evidence currently links Roland C. Nathaniels’s Zonophone 3972 “Ewe Battle Hymn” or “Anlo Patriotic Song” to haló. Their catalogue titles and session date alone do not establish thematic eligibility.

### Research action / next evidence threshold

Search for the colonial notice or correspondence mentioned by Campbell, record its exact date, issuing authority, archival repository, file reference and wording; then seek any pre-1926 *haló* song text or named composer/performer that can be matched to a commercial recording. The Ghana Public Records and Archives Administration Department lists Secretary for Native Affairs holdings (ADM 11) among its collections, but no item-level *haló* file has been identified. PRAAD collection guide: https://praad.gov.gh/index.php/popular-collection-chieftaincy/ . Until a recording/text link is established, this is contextual evidence and a search strategy, not a candidate recording.

Add *haló* as a high-priority Anlo-Ewe grievance-song tradition for targeted recording discovery. Do not promote any Nathaniels side or add a recording candidate on the strength of this context alone.
