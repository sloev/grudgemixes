# African early-recording leads: South Africa 1923 and Anlo-Ewe 1925

**Date:** 2026-11-15  
**Status:** Screening note; neither item is yet commercially cleared.  
**Scope:** Two commercial Zonophone leads from the Gramophone Company's London recording activity, kept distinct from ethnographic field recordings.

## 1. Sol T. Plaatje — “A Band of Hard-Pressed Men Are We” (Zonophone 4169, 1923)

**Status: PARTIALLY VERIFIED / STRONG TEXTUAL LEAD / AUDIO PROVENANCE AND RIGHTS UNRESOLVED**

### Discographic identity

- Performer: Solomon Tshekisho (Sol) Plaatje (1876–1932), vocal.
- Language: isiXhosa (historical sources often spell “Si-Xosa”).
- Title: “A Band of Hard-Pressed Men Are We”; Flat International discography gives the Xhosa-language performance title in English translation.
- Label/catalogue: Zonophone 4169; side number X-7-32087.
- Matrix/take: Bb3641-3.
- Recording session: Hayes, Middlesex, UK; 23 October 1923 (the earlier 16 October session was retaken; the third take was issued).
- Piano: Mr. Grant and Madame Adami are identified in Siemon Allen's discographical reconstruction for the 23 October retake; the original 16 October Plaatje session used Sylvia Colenso.
- Coupled side: “The Kaffir Wedding Song” (John Knox Bokwe), matrix Bb3642-3, Zonophone 4169 / X-7-32088.
- Plaatje was in Britain as a member and former general secretary of the South African Native National Congress (SANNC, later ANC); he had previously travelled to protest the 1913 Natives Land Act. This is relevant biography, but does **not** prove that this hymn is a protest song about the Act.

### Text and authorship: important correction to the apparent political reading

The English hymn text is documented in *The Fellowship Hymn Book* (1908), no.19, as **“A Band of Hard-Pressed Men”**, by **Edwin Gilbert**, set to **“ALSACE”**, attributed to Beethoven. The identity/dates of the hymn author need a small qualification: Hymnary's text page cites **Edwin Gilbert, 1859-**, its linked authority record gives **1848–1926**, and another Hymnary search result gives **1859–c.1935**. The birth-year discrepancy should be retained pending authoritative biographical resolution; all of these records place the author's death before the project's 1956 cutoff. Beethoven died in 1827. The text itself is securely published in 1908.

The original English text makes the thematic content more precise than the title alone suggests. It speaks of a hard-pressed collective, hardship, “bitterness of heart,” “hate and want of charity,” daily bread and fear; but the prayer asks for the better part, charity, love and strength to face life's strife. It is a Christian hymn of shared hardship and moral self-examination, not a call to retaliate against an identified adversary. The recorded isiXhosa text appears to be a translation/adaptation of this hymn. Plaatje's translation work is documented in the same period: Siemon Allen notes that Plaatje had returned to Britain partly to seek funding to publish his translation of the Fellowship Hymn Book used by the International Brotherhood Congress. The precise translator credit for this particular Xhosa version should still be checked against the manuscript/hymnbook.

**Provisional taxonomy:** collective hardship / acknowledged bitterness and resentment / religious consolation. It is relevant to a broad grudge-and-resentment anthology as a counterpoint about the naming and disciplining of bitterness, but it is not spite, retaliation or revenge on current evidence. Do not frame it as a coded anti-Land Act song without lyric or contextual evidence.

### Audio, archival source and rights

- Wits University Historical Papers Research Archive lists the Plaatje recording collection (ZA HPRA A1742) and a digitised item A1742-1 containing the songs; the item is catalogued under **16 October 1923**. This date must not be assigned automatically to every track: Flat International's matrix-based reconstruction says the two later sides were retaken on **23 October 1923**, and identifies the issued Bb3641-3 take of “A Band of Hard-Pressed Men Are We” as that later session. The Wits item may group material from both sessions, but this has not been proven. Its collection description also records a physical damaged Zonophone 4168 disc in the Plaatje papers; the exact original source and take of the digitised audio need confirmation.
- Flat International's South African Audio Archive reproduces label images and discographic reconstruction, and identifies the 4169 matrices/takes. It is a research/discography source, not itself proof that an accessible digital file is an unedited archival transfer.
- A reissue or tape copy must not be treated as the required raw archival transfer of the original shellac. Obtain Wits/other archive item-level transfer notes and the original 4169 disc provenance.
- Rights to the 1908 hymn text and Beethoven tune may be public domain, but the 1923 sound recording, performance, translation, and archival digital transfer must be assessed separately by territory and intended use. **No commercial clearance established.**

## 2. Roland C. Nathaniels — “Ewe Battle Hymn” (Zonophone 3972, 1925)

**Status: HIGH-PRIORITY LEAD / NOT YET A CANDIDATE / NOT CLEARED**

### Discographic identity

- Performer: Roland C. Nathaniels, Anlo-Ewe musician from Keta, described by Ernest Owusu-Poku as a trained composer, pianist and organist.
- Language: Ewe.
- Title: “Ewe Battle Hymn.”
- Label/catalogue: Zonophone 3972 (10-inch, 78rpm shellac).
- Side number: X-4-42857.
- Matrix/take: Yy7339-2.
- Recording date: 20 November 1925, in the London-based Gramophone Company/Zonophone West African recording campaign.
- Discography describes the item as **“Native melody”** (unaccompanied in the catalogue entry). That is a useful provenance clue but is not, by itself, sufficient proof that the composition is traditional or that the recording reproduces an established Anlo war-song text.
- The coupled side is “Anlo Patriotic Song,” X-4-42856 / matrix Yy7340-1, recorded the same day; it is described as Ewe, song with piano and whistling chorus.

### Historical and genre context

Owusu-Poku's study documents Nathaniels's 1925 recording contract and lists Zonophone 3972 as “Anlo patriotic song & Ewe battle hymn.” The same study says the Anlo Patriotic Song was later published as sheet music by Eclipse Music Publishing Company in Britain on 1 January 1926. That publication date is after the project's cutoff, although the master was fixed on 20 November 1925.

Research on Anlo-Ewe musical traditions describes war songs as repertoire sung by warriors before, during and after battle: they can encourage courage, dare opponents, expose an enemy's weaknesses, recount victories and preserve military memory. Some Anlo war-song genres are tied to particular dance-drumming practices (including Kpegisu and Atrikpi). This is relevant cultural context, but no source yet identifies the 1925 “Ewe Battle Hymn” as a particular Kpegisu/Atrikpi song, nor connects its lyrics to a named historical battle or revenge episode.

**Provisional taxonomy:** battle / collective resistance / martial memory. Whether it contains revenge, retaliation, grievance or merely martial exhortation is unknown until the Ewe text is recovered and translated with competent language assistance. Do not equate “battle hymn” with revenge.

### Authorship, audio and rights

- The discography's “Native melody” description suggests a traditional melody, but composition/lyric provenance and the exact relation of Nathaniels's arrangement to any older song remain open.
- Nathaniels's own dates and the authorship of any lyrics are not established in the sources consulted. The recording's 1925 fixation date meets the recording-date cutoff; the composition/traditional-provenance gate is not yet proven.
- The 1925 disc is a commercial shellac issue, not a field recording. No original copy, item-level archive transfer, direct audio, or transfer notes have yet been identified. Later compilations/reissues are discovery/listening leads only and do not establish an eligible raw transfer.
- Commercial rights to composition, performance, master and archival transfer remain unresolved.

### Separate related title: “Anlo Patriotic Song”

Zonophone 3972's other side, “Anlo Patriotic Song,” has matrix Yy7340-1 and was also recorded on 20 November 1925. Owusu-Poku reports that it was later issued as sheet music on 1 January 1926. Do not merge it with “Ewe Battle Hymn”: it is a distinct song, described as a piano-accompanied song with whistling chorus, and its authorship/lyrics and cutoff eligibility require separate checks. It may be a relevant collective-political expression, but no revenge mechanism has been established.

## Screening decisions

- **Plaatje, “A Band of Hard-Pressed Men Are We”:** PARTIALLY VERIFIED / strong textual lead. Authorship, pre-cutoff hymn publication, commercial issue identity and historical recording session are documented; Xhosa translation credit, item-level raw transfer and rights remain open. The text concerns hardship and bitterness but advocates charity and love, not revenge.
- **Nathaniels, “Ewe Battle Hymn”:** HIGH-PRIORITY LEAD. Exact 1925 recording date, catalogue number, matrix/take and Ewe language are documented; traditional provenance, lyric meaning, audio source and rights remain open.
- **Nathaniels, “Anlo Patriotic Song”:** SEPARATE LEAD. 1925 master fixed before cutoff; later sheet music published 1 January 1926; authorship and lyrics need review.


## 3. Ladipo Solanke — Yoruba-language Zonophone 78s (November–December 1925)

**Status: RESEARCH CLUSTER / INDIVIDUAL TITLES UNRESOLVED**

Afrodisc's discography gives a detailed session-level sequence for Ladipo Solanke (Omo Lisabi), whose Yoruba-language recordings were made in London in 1925. Solanke was a Nigerian student activist and co-founder of the West African Students' Union; reliable biographical sources give his death as **2 September 1958**, after the composition-author cutoff. His own authorship therefore cannot satisfy the 1956 rule if he composed any particular item; a traditional provenance would need to be established separately.

### The strongest thematic title: “Orin Ofo” (Zonophone 3963)

- Title as catalogued: **ORIN OFO** (Yoruba; preserve unmarked source spelling).
- Disc: Zonophone 3963, side X-4-42843.
- Matrix/take: Yy7461-1.
- Recording date: 7 December 1925.
- Performer: Ladipo Solanke (Omo Lisabi).
- Afrodisc classifies the side as **“Lament.”**
- A historical Yoruba dictionary glosses *ofo* as “loss, calamity, damage”; a modern dictionary also records senses related to emptiness/loneliness. Tone marks and regional usage matter: the unmarked catalogue spelling cannot by itself settle the precise lexical reading. Afrodisc's “Lament” is the current genre description, not a full translation of the lyrics.

This is a promising **grief / loss / collective-lament lead**, but not yet a grievance, retaliation or revenge item. Need a Yoruba-language transcript, competent translation, context for the lament, and proof whether the text is traditional or authored by Solanke/another named writer. Solanke's 1958 death makes that authorship distinction consequential for eligibility.

### “Yoruba Philosophy” (Zonophone 3961): do not misread “Oriki Lara” as envy

- Zonophone 3961, side X-41185, matrix Yy7457-1, recorded 7 December 1925: **YORUBA PHILOSOPHY Part 1 (ORIKI LARA)**, catalogued as spoken.
- Coupled side: Part 2 (ORIKI ERIN), X-41186 / Yy7458-1, same session.
- Smithsonian's *Black Europe* catalogue renders the first subtitle as “Oriki ilara,” but Afrodisc gives “Oriki Lara.” Yoruba *oríkì* is a praise poem / praise-name discourse, not simply a generic “song”; the spelling may refer to a proper name or other phrase. Do **not** translate this as “praise of envy” or classify it as jealousy without a source text and Yoruba language review.
- Status: spoken-word discovery lead only; exact content, textual provenance and translation unknown.

### Other relevant sides from the same 7 December 1925 session

- **“Orin Imokanle”** — Zonophone 3963, X-4-42844, matrix Yy7462-1; catalogue says “Song.” Title and text not yet interpreted.
- **“Yoruba Diplomacy in a Comic Air”** — Zonophone 3962, X-4-42841, matrix Yy7459-2; catalogue says “Song.” Possible satire/social conflict lead, but no content verified.
- **“Yoruba Lesson of Obedience to Parents”** — Zonophone 3962, X-4-42842, matrix Yy7460-1; catalogue says “Ballad.” A moral/narrative lead, no grievance content verified.
- **“Yoruba Moral Code in Aphorisms”**, parts 1–2 — Zonophone 3964, X-41187 / X-41188, matrices Yy7463-1 / Yy7464-1; spoken.
- **“Customs and Laws in Proverbs”**, parts 1–2 — Zonophone 3965, X-41189 / X-41190, matrices Yy7469-1 / Yy7470-2; spoken.

The earlier 4 November 1925 sessions include “Orin Ijoba Egba” (Zonophone 3953, X-4-42828 / Yy7178-2), “Oriki Ege” (3954, X-4-42829 / Yy7180-1), and “Orin Oloti” (3954, X-4-42830 / Yy7181-1). These are discovery leads only; do not infer meaning from unmarked transliteration.

### Wider context and access

- Solanke recorded Yoruba songs, praise poetry, proverbs, ballads and spoken material as part of a deliberate effort to preserve and promote Yoruba language/culture in Britain. He was also a political organiser; that biography is not proof that every recorded item is political protest.
- The Smithsonian's *Black Europe* discography confirms the Solanke cluster appears in the historical compilation; the commercial compilation is a secondary reissue, not a qualifying raw transfer.
- Afrodisc supplies matrix/take and date information but is a specialist secondary discography. The Gramophone Company/EMI archive or an original Zonophone copy is needed to verify label details, issue history and raw transfer.
- No raw archival transfer, exact lyrics, composition credits or commercial rights have been established.
