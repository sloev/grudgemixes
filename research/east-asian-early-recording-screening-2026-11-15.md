# East Asian early-recording screening — Korea and Cantonese/Chinese records (2026-11-15)

Status: **regional screening / no new candidate admitted**.

## Korea: Fletcher cylinders (1896) and the disputed 1899 recording event

The strongest securely documented early Korean audio object surfaced in this pass is the Alice Cunningham Fletcher collection: six Edison wax cylinders recorded in Washington, D.C., on 24 July 1896, with Korean students Ahn Jeong-sik, Lee Hee-Cheol and Son Rong. The Korean History Net account describes roughly 17 minutes of recordings and lists “Maehwa taryeong,” an “Aegukga” in folk style, “Arirang” (Haeju Arong taryeong), “Jebiga,” “Dala dala,” and a hand-clapping game. The Library of Congress confirms Fletcher's date and performers and describes these as early ethnographic recordings. They are **field/academic recordings**, not commercial releases. None of the song titles or catalogue descriptions presently establishes revenge, spite or retaliation; the patriotic and folk materials should not be classified by title alone.

A discrepancy emerged around the often-repeated 1899 Gamunjeong Pavilion event in Seoul. The National Gugak Center's online exhibition says government officials recorded *Chunhyang-ga*, gasa and japga there in 1899. However, the Korean History Net account states that no surviving Korean cylinder recordings from the period had yet been found, and identifies the six 1896 Fletcher cylinders as the oldest located recordings. Until an exact surviving 1899 cylinder, inventory or archival audio object is identified, the 1899 event is **historical recording testimony, not a verified extant master**.

*Chunhyang-ga* may warrant a textual search because its narrative includes the corrupt magistrate Byeon Hak-do's abuse of Chunhyang and his eventual punishment when Yi Mong-ryong returns in an official capacity. That is a possible **punishment / justice** mechanism in a literary narrative, not automatically revenge; moreover, the claimed 1899 recording is not yet an identified surviving audio object. The 1937 Okeh *Chunhyangjeon* set is far outside the cutoff and cannot stand in for the 1899 event.

## Cantonese / Chinese recording infrastructure and item leads

The Chinese Historical Society of Southern California's Phonograph Record Collection contains about 250 records, mostly Cantonese (Yueju) opera on 10-inch shellac discs, produced in the first half of the 20th century in China, England, Germany and the United States. The finding aid includes at least one dated early item: Victor Talking Machine Co. 1769, catalogued as **入仙賀寿**, Camden, New Jersey, 1902, a 10-inch 78rpm disc sent for digitization. The item has a digital-material link, but its performer, dialect, matrix, precise recording date, lyrics and relevance to grievance are not established in the finding-aid entry. Treat it as an archival object to inspect, not as a candidate.

A separate title-level lead in the same collection is **梨娘恨** (catalogued with performer Bao Yu / 寶玉; Golden Star G-S 39 A/B and G-S 39 C/D). The title contains 恨 (“hatred/resentment”), but the record is undated and the lyric/plot is unknown. This is a **title lead only**, not evidence of a grudge or revenge song. Another Golden Star item, **滿天神佛**, is also undated and not grievance evidence.

For the early commercial industry, Yung Sai-shing's study reports that the Gramophone Company and Edison began producing Cantonese songs, operatic songs, ballads and folk songs in 1903. The Gramophone Company Discography's 430,000-plus entry database is a key way to search its global matrices and issue numbers, but the underlying title/person fields are inconsistent and must be cross-checked against catalogues and labels. A specialist discographical summary describes Gaisberg's March–April 1903 Chinese expedition as 476 records: 329 Mandarin-dialect titles recorded in Shanghai and 147 Cantonese-dialect titles recorded in Hong Kong. This is a search map, not candidate evidence.

## Greek chronology caution

A Cambridge note on rebetiko chronology warns that dates assigned to pre-war recordings are often unreliable and not based on factual evidence. Any Greek/Ottoman urban song discovered through later rebetiko discographies must be checked against matrix chronology, original catalogues, labels and session documentation. Do not treat a modern discography's date as publication or fixation proof.

- Ole L. Smith, “The chronology of Rebetiko — a reconsideration of the evidence” (1991; online 2016): https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/byzantine-and-modern-greek-studies/article/abs/chronology-of-rebetiko-a-reconsideration-of-the-evidence/D5BFC4A14B02375A1ED58D5135839935

## Japan: Gishi / Chūshingura rōkyoku recordings — high-value new lead

The International Research Center for Japanese Studies (Nichibunken) maintains a dedicated **Rōkyoku (Naniwabushi) SP Record Digital Archive**. Its stated corpus is 9,998 SP records donated by collector Morikawa Tsukasa, published from the Meiji 30s through the Showa 30s; the interface includes record-surface images, audio and related materials. The archive says all disc images are open online, while audio is openly playable only where the copyright term has expired; other audio is available only on-site. It digitises playback using a laser turntable, phono equalizer and analogue-to-digital converter. This is an unusually strong research path because the archive exposes label/catalogue images and a direct searchable database, though the web search interface itself returned 403 in this session.

Henry D. Smith's study of the Akō Gishi states that the first Gishi tale record was issued in **August 1910**, with many more following in the next years. He explains that three-minute sides generally captured selected song/story segments, not a complete extended tale; a record title may therefore represent one episode within a larger revenge story. The Akō vendetta itself is a historical event (1701–03), while many later *Gishiden* episodes include fictional characters and accretions. Do not treat every episode as a transparent record of the historical event.

The same archive's search-indexed entries surfaced several later *Gishi* items, useful as proof that this corpus is searchable by episode title but **not yet pre-1926 candidates**:
- **(義士傳) 安兵衞變裝** (*Gishiden: Yasubee hensō*), Tōchūken Jōun, Regal / Columbia 65232–65233, issued January 1933; archive record 519216.
- **(義士外傳) 大竹重兵衛** (*Gishi gaiden: Ōtake Jūbei*), Kiyomi Tsuda, Augon A5178, issue date not supplied; archive record 517460.
- **義士引揚 / 義士最後** (*Gishi hikiage / Gishi saigo*), Yoshida Naramaru, Nipponophone 1891–1892; CiNii record BB17665563 gives no issue year, so it cannot be assumed eligible.
- **赤穂義士 神嵜與五郎 (一)/(二)**, Yoshida Naramaru, Parrot 412/413; a specialist Japanese label list gives no new-issue date. Lead only.
- A 78music label list also shows **義士討入** (*Gishi uchiiri*, “Gishi attack/raid”), Yoshida Naramaru, Special Record, and **殿中刃傷** (“wounding in the palace”), but without catalogue/date evidence.

A separate Japanese legal-history source identifies a particularly important 1912 commercial group by **Tōchūken Kumoemon (1873–1916)**: “Akagaki Genzo Tokkuri no Wakare,” “Nambuzaka Goshitsu Yuki no Wakare,” “Oishi Oitachi,” “Murakami Kiken,” and “Masamune Koshiden,” sold on 19 May 1912 after a high-profile exclusive recording contract. These titles belong to the Akō Gishi story-cycle, but none should be labelled a revenge song until the exact side's words/episode are established. In particular, *Oishi Oitachi* is biographical prehistory and *Murakami Kiken* concerns a fictional character in later Gishiden storytelling. Kumoemon's death in 1916 is within the project's author/performer date threshold, but the improvised/performative status of Naniwabushi and any distinct text/arrangement credits need separate examination.

### Kumoemon's 1911 session / 1912 Lyrophone release — stronger discographic sub-lead

Further source triangulation has produced an exact, well-dated commercial group, although not yet an archival audio object:
- Takashi Otani's J-STAGE article says Tōchūken Kumoemon recorded four named performances in the Sanko-do studio in **December 1911**: **赤垣源蔵（徳利の別れ）** (*Akagaki Genzo: Tokkuri no Wakare*), **大石生立（御薬献上）** (*Oishi Oitachi: Goyaku Kenjō*), **村上喜剣** (*Murakami Kiken*), and **正宗孝子伝** (*Masamune Kōshiden*). Each performance occupied both sides of a disc. It says the copyright was registered on 28 February 1912 and transferred to Richard Werdermann.
- Yoshihisa Nomi's legal-history study provides a more detailed production and issue account: the recording engineer travelled from Germany; the masters were made in Tokyo, shipped to Germany, manufactured by Lyrophon and imported for Sanko-do distribution. It identifies five selections in the first release group, adding **南部坂後室の別れ / 南部坂後室雪の別れ** (*Nambuzaka Goshitsu no Wakare / Yuki no Wakare*) to the list above. The first group was advertised for sale in May 1912 (newspaper advertisements dated 18 and 31 May); the source describes a second group of five released on 18 November 1912. The first five discs cost 3 yen 80 sen each. The precise relationship between the J-STAGE four-title account and Nomi's five-title account is unresolved; preserve this discrepancy rather than silently reconciling it.
- The 1912 first-group label evidence reproduced by Nomi includes **LYROPHONE-RECORD / STARKTON** and a Japanese title label for *Murakami Kiken*; the paper reports the five original titles and a photographed label. This is stronger than a modern reissue discography for issue identity.
- A 2017 Osaka Kyoiku University study analyses the text/performance structure of Kumoemon's **南部坂後室 雪の別れ**, gives an opening lyric excerpt and identifies its source as a **1912 Lyrophone** record. The episode concerns Ōishi's visit to the widow/household of Asano before the attack, not the raid itself. It is a pre-revenge farewell / planning episode within the Gishi story-cycle; classification should remain **represented revenge context / collective grievance**, not automatically a standalone revenge lyric, until the full text is examined.
- A 2017 study of the **赤垣源蔵 徳利の別れ** recording lineage identifies Kumoemon's Lyrophone E1009 (1912), alongside later recordings. CiNii lists the Parlophone issue E1009 (70937–70938) and E1010 (70939–70940), but that catalogue entry itself gives no issue year; use the 1912 newspaper/production evidence, not the Parlophone reissue metadata, for dating the original.
- Kumoemon died in 1916. This satisfies the performer/reciter death-date threshold only if the project's composition rule treats the performed narration as his authored/improvised work; the Nomi paper describes the recording rights dispute as concerning his particular narrative performance, and historical Japanese legal analysis debates whether the improvised *naniwabushi* qualified as a stable musical work. Do not presume traditional provenance or authorship without identifying the text/reciter credit for each episode.

- Performer: 桃中軒雲右衛門入道, Kumoemonnyūdō Tōchūken.
- Label: Lyrophone Record.
- Record numbers: **70937–70940**, a two-disc set / four sides.
- Release month: **May 1912**.
- Archive object ID: **519319**.
- The entry says the original record was digitised with a laser turntable, phono equalizer and analogue-to-digital converter; its access status is internet-open because the archive publishes recordings whose copyright term has expired.
- A companion Kumoemon *Akagaki Genzo (Kinshu no kudari)* is archive ID **519351**, Lyrophone 70920–70921, November 1912.

The metadata search result is accessible, but direct opening of the record page returned HTTP 403 in this research session. Thus we have verified the archive's description of the digital transfer and public-access status, but have **not personally listened to the file or inspected the disc images**. Do not describe this as an audio-content verification.

The J-STAGE article gives a somewhat different first-session list: Kumoemon recorded four narratives at Sanko-do in December 1911, each across both sides of a disc; the list includes *Akagaki Genzo (Tokkuri no Wakare)*, *Oishi Oitachi (Goyaku Kenjō)*, *Murakami Kiken* and *Masamune Kōshiden*. It says Kumoemon registered the performance copyright and transferred it to Richard Werdermann on 28 February 1912. The later Nichibunken archive entry provides the exact label number/release date for *Akagaki Genzo*. The court case is especially important for the project's composition gate: the Supreme Court ultimately held that the recorded Naniwabushi was an improvised performance and not a copyrightable literary work under the law then in force; the 2013 J-STAGE author nevertheless argues that by modern criteria the performer could be considered an author because Naniwabushi performers shaped lyrics, dialogue and melody within a known plot. Keep historical legal doctrine distinct from present-day editorial assessment of authorship.

### B. Yoshida Naramaru, *Gishi no Uchiiri / Gishi no Hikiage* — stronger thematic lead, 1923

A specialist Japanese Victor/Nipponophone catalogue list identifies a two-sided 78rpm issue:
- **義士の討入** (*Gishi no Uchiiri*, “The Gishi raid”) — Yoshida Naramaru, Nipponophone **15139-A**.
- **義士の引揚** (*Gishi no Hikiage*, “The Gishi withdrawal/return”) — Yoshida Naramaru, Nipponophone **15139-B**.
- New-issue month: **July 1923**.
- Genre: Naniwabushi.
This is the most directly revenge-centred Japanese title surfaced so far: the A-side explicitly narrates the raid on Kira's residence, the retaliatory act at the centre of the Akō vendetta. The B-side follows the withdrawal/return. A separate historical-label catalogue also lists the pair under Yoshida Naramaru.

A pre-existing textual witness strengthens the repertoire context: the National Diet Library's 1912 *Gishi no Hitofushi: Naniwabushi Jūhachiban* (吉田奈良丸講演; 2nd Yoshida Naramaru, 1883–1967) contains a contents entry **義士吉良邸討入** (“Gishi raid on Kira's mansion”) and **義士の本懐を遂する條** (“the episode in which the Gishi fulfil their purpose”). The scanned text is from the 1912 *Hana no maki / Yuki no maki* volumes. This is a dated printed witness to the same traditional narrative episode, but it does not prove word-for-word identity with the 1923 recording. Yoshida Naramaru (2nd) died in 1967, so performer death alone does not satisfy the author's-death cutoff. The eligibility argument must rest on the **traditional narrative/performance repertoire with provenance**, not on an unsupported claim that the performer died before 1956.

The underlying Akō incident (1701–03) is historical, but the familiar forty-seven-rōnin story was extensively shaped by theatre, kōdan and Naniwabushi. Treat the recording as a **traditional narrative representation of collective revenge**, not an unmediated historical transcript. The 1912 printed contents establish the raid episode's place in Naramaru's repertoire; exact recorded lyrics and the degree of traditional versus performer-specific authorship remain open.

### C. Earlier Yamato On-ei recording evidence — by June 1914

A significant earlier source has surfaced in Hidenori Onishi's 2023 Ritsumeikan University study of the Kyoto record company Tōyō Chikuonki / Orient Record. The article reproduces a newspaper report from **Osaka Mainichi, Kyoto-Shiga supplement, 19 June 1914**. The report says Tokyo's **Yamato On-ei** company had commissioned Yoshida Naramaru to record and was selling under its **馬印 (horse-mark) label** the Naniwabushi selections **義士の討入** (*Gishi no Uchiiri*), **源蔵徳利の別れ** (*Genzo Tokkuri no Wakare*) and **南部坂** (*Nanbuzaka*), described as his signature pieces. The same report says a Kyoto company copied these records and sold the copies under a rival mark, prompting a trademark investigation. The article reproduces a photograph of the original Yamato On-ei Yoshida Naramaru record label (Figure 5).

This establishes that a commercial **Yoshida Naramaru recording titled Gishi no Uchiiri existed and was being sold no later than 19 June 1914**—earlier than the 1923 Nipponophone 15139-A listing. It is not yet enough to date the exact fixation or identify its matrix/catalogue number; it may be the original recording later re-recorded or reissued by Nipponophone, but that relationship must not be assumed. The 1914 newspaper account is contemporaneous evidence of sale, while Onishi's 2023 paper is the accessible reproduction and contextual source.

The 1912 printed *Gishi no Hitofushi: Naniwabushi Jūhachiban* by Yoshida Naramaru (2nd) contains a table-of-contents entry for **義士吉良邸討入** and a separate episode **義士の本懐を遂する條**. Its colophon gives printing on 1 March and publication on 5 March 1912. This makes a pre-1926 textual witness for Naramaru's raid episode available two years before the 1914 commercial recording report. It is a repertoire witness, not yet a word-for-word transcript of the recorded side.

The 1914 report names only “Yoshida Naramaru.” Given the active recording career of the second Naramaru (1879–1967), he is the probable performer, but the label photograph and original disc should be checked before making the identification definitive. Do not confuse him with the first Yoshida Naramaru (1851–1915), who had retired from the name in 1902. The 1912 book's NDL/Wikimedia metadata explicitly identifies the second Naramaru (1883–1967 in the NDL authority record; biographical sources often give 1879), so date of birth variants need a note, but all sources place his death in 1967.

Decision refinement: **Gishi no Uchiiri** is now a stronger high-priority lead with contemporaneous evidence of commercial sale by June 1914, a pictured Yamato On-ei label, and a 1912 printed Naramaru repertoire witness. It remains **LEAD / NOT YET CANDIDATE** until the original Yamato disc is identified (label/catalogue/matrix), the 1914 performer identity is confirmed, and the recorded text is compared with the 1912 printed narrative. The 1923 Nipponophone 15139-A may be a later separate recording; do not collapse the two issues.


### D. Takamine Chikufu, *Gishi no Uchiiri* — 1924 Nipponophone and undated Menophone holdings

A second early recorded treatment of the raid appears in the specialist Nipponophone catalogue:
- **義士討入（一）（二）**, then **義士討入（三）（四）**, Takamine Chikufu (高峰筑風), label Nipponophone, catalogue numbers **15226–15227**, new-issue date **January 1924**, genre **Takamine biwa** (高峰琵琶). This is a four-side performance, not the same recording as Yoshida Naramaru's Naniwabushi side 15139-A.
- Takamine Chikufu (Suzuki Tetsurō, 1879–1936) founded the Takamine biwa school in 1912 and was a recognized biwa performer/composer. His death is before 1956, but the catalogue does not credit him as lyricist/composer for this particular *Gishi no Uchiiri*. Do not assume his performer credit automatically satisfies the author/composer rule.
- A 1981 Kushiro Municipal Museum catalogue of the Tokuji Sakai collection independently lists a physical four-side **義士の討入（一）〜（四）** by Takamine Chikufu on **Menophone 1618–1621**. This may be a different earlier issue or another label/version, but the museum catalogue gives no publication date and no matrix or transfer information. It is a real collection-level object, not proof of pre-1926 publication. The same collection lists other Akō-repertoire discs, including **続義士の本懐** (Toyoda Kyokusuì, Nipponophone 379–380) and **義士の本懐** (Toyoda Kyokusuì, Nipponophone 1799–1800), also without dates in this inventory.
- A separate 2020 study of modern biwa SP releases provides a broad catalogue framework and describes the growth of modern biwa schools and repertoire. It notes that the record market drew on historically popular narrative subjects, but the surfaced passages do not assign authorship of the 1924 *Gishi no Uchiiri* to Takamine.

Decision: **PARTIALLY DOCUMENTED DISC / LEAD, NOT CANDIDATE**. The 1924 Nipponophone issue is a strong date/title/performer lead and a four-side raid narrative. The Menophone 1618–1621 holdings are an important physical-disc lead but remain undated. Composition authorship/traditional provenance, exact archival audio/transfer and commercial rights remain unresolved. Keep this separate from Naramaru's 1914 Yamato On-ei and 1923 Nipponophone versions.
