# East Asian early-recording screening — Korea and Cantonese/Chinese records (2026-11-15)

Status: **regional screening / no new candidate admitted**.

## Korea: Fletcher cylinders (1896) and the disputed 1899 recording event

The strongest securely documented early Korean audio object surfaced in this pass is the Alice Cunningham Fletcher collection: six Edison wax cylinders recorded in Washington, D.C., on 24 July 1896, with Korean students Ahn Jeong-sik, Lee Hee-Cheol and Son Rong. The Korean History Net account describes roughly 17 minutes of recordings and lists “Maehwa taryeong,” an “Aegukga” in folk style, “Arirang” (Haeju Arong taryeong), “Jebiga,” “Dala dala,” and a hand-clapping game. The Library of Congress confirms Fletcher's date and performers and describes these as early ethnographic recordings. They are **field/academic recordings**, not commercial releases. None of the song titles or catalogue descriptions presently establishes revenge, spite or retaliation; the patriotic and folk materials should not be classified by title alone.

A discrepancy emerged around the often-repeated 1899 Gamunjeong Pavilion event in Seoul. The National Gugak Center's online exhibition says government officials recorded *Chunhyang-ga*, gasa and japga there in 1899. However, the Korean History Net account states that no surviving Korean cylinder recordings from the period had yet been found, and identifies the six 1896 Fletcher cylinders as the oldest located recordings. Until an exact surviving 1899 cylinder, inventory or archival audio object is identified, the 1899 event is **historical recording testimony, not a verified extant master**.

*Chunhyang-ga* may warrant a textual search because its narrative includes the corrupt magistrate Byeon Hak-do's abuse of Chunhyang and his eventual punishment when Yi Mong-ryong returns in an official capacity. That is a possible **punishment / justice** mechanism in a literary narrative, not automatically revenge; moreover, the claimed 1899 recording is not yet an identified surviving audio object. The 1937 Okeh *Chunhyangjeon* set is far outside the cutoff and cannot stand in for the 1899 event.

## Cantonese / Chinese recording infrastructure and item leads

The Chinese Historical Society of Southern California's Phonograph Record Collection contains about 250 records, mostly Cantonese (Yueju) opera on 10-inch shellac discs, produced in the first half of the 20th century in China, England, Germany and the United States. The finding aid includes at least one dated early item: Victor Talking Machine Co. 1769, catalogued as **入仙賀寿**, Camden, New Jersey, 1902, a 10-inch 78rpm disc sent for digitization. The item has a digital-material link, but its performer, dialect, matrix, precise recording date, lyrics and relevance to grievance are not established in the finding-aid entry. Treat it as an archival object to inspect, not as a candidate.

A separate title-level lead in the same collection is **梨娘恨** (catalogued with performer Bao Yu / 寶玉; Golden Star G-S 39 A/B and G-S 39 C/D). The title contains 恨 (“hatred/resentment”), but the record is undated and the lyric/plot is unknown. This is a **title lead only**, not evidence of a grudge or revenge song. Another Golden Star item, **滿天神佛**, is also undated and not grievance evidence.

For the early commercial industry, Yung Sai-shing's study reports that the Gramophone Company and Edison began producing Cantonese songs, operatic songs, ballads and folk songs in 1903. The Gramophone Company Discography's 430,000-plus entry database is a key way to search its global matrices and issue numbers, but the underlying title/person fields are inconsistent and must be cross-checked against catalogues and labels. A specialist discographical summary describes Gaisberg's March–April 1903 Chinese expedition as 476 records: 329 Mandarin-dialect titles recorded in Shanghai and 147 Cantonese-dialect titles recorded in Hong Kong. This is a search map, not candidate evidence.

## Greek chronology caution

A Cambridge note on rebetiko chronology warns that dates assigned to pre-war recordings are often unreliable and not based on factual evidence. Any Greek/Ottoman urban song discovered through later rebetiko discographies must be checked against matrix chronology, original catalogues, labels and session documentation. Do not treat a modern discography's date as publication or fixation proof.

- Ole L. Smith, “The chronology of Rebetiko — a reconsideration of the evidence” (1991; online 2016): https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/byzantine-and-modern-greek-studies/article/abs/chronology-of-rebetiko-a-reconsideration-of-the-evidence/D5BFC4A14B02375A1ED58D5135839935

## Japan: Gishi / Chūshingura rōkyoku recordings — high-value new lead

The International Research Center for Japanese Studies (Nichibunken) maintains a dedicated **Rōkyoku (Naniwabushi) SP Record Digital Archive**. Its stated corpus is 9,998 SP records donated by collector Morikawa Tsukasa, published from the Meiji 30s through the Showa 30s; the interface includes record-surface images, audio and related materials. The archive says all disc images are open online, while audio is openly playable only where the copyright term has expired; other audio is available only on-site. It digitises playback using a laser turntable, phono equalizer and analogue-to-digital converter. This is an unusually strong research path because the archive exposes label/catalogue images and a direct searchable database, though the web search interface itself returned 403 in this session.

Henry D. Smith's study of the Akō Gishi states that the first Gishi tale record was issued in **August 1910**, with many more following in the next years. He explains that three-minute sides generally captured selected song/story segments, not a complete extended tale; a record title may therefore represent one episode within a larger revenge story. The Akō vendetta itself is a historical event (1701–03), while many later *Gishiden* episodes include fictional characters and accretions. Do not treat every episode as a transparent record of the historical event.

The same archive's search-indexed entries surfaced several later *Gishi* items, useful as proof that this corpus is searchable by episode title but **not yet pre-1926 candidates**:
- **(義士傳) 安兵衞變裝** (*Gishiden: Yasubee hensō*), Tōchūken Jōun, Regal / Columbia 65232–65233, issued January 1933; archive record 519216.
- **(義士外傳) 大竹重兵衛** (*Gishi gaiden: Ōtake Jūbei*), Kiyomi Tsuda, Augon A5178, issue date not supplied; archive record 517460.
- **義士引揚 / 義士最後** (*Gishi hikiage / Gishi saigo*), Yoshida Naramaru, Nipponophone 1891–1892; CiNii record BB17665563 gives no issue year, so it cannot be assumed eligible.
- **赤穂義士 神嵜與五郎 (一)/(二)**, Yoshida Naramaru, Parrot 412/413; a specialist Japanese label list gives no new-issue date. Lead only.
- A 78music label list also shows **義士討入** (*Gishi uchiiri*, “Gishi attack/raid”), Yoshida Naramaru, Special Record, and **殿中刃傷** (“wounding in the palace”), but without catalogue/date evidence.

A separate Japanese legal-history source identifies a particularly important 1912 commercial group by **Tōchūken Kumoemon (1873–1916)**: “Akagaki Genzo Tokkuri no Wakare,” “Nambuzaka Goshitsu Yuki no Wakare,” “Oishi Oitachi,” “Murakami Kiken,” and “Masamune Koshiden,” sold on 19 May 1912 after a high-profile exclusive recording contract. These titles belong to the Akō Gishi story-cycle, but none should be labelled a revenge song until the exact side's words/episode are established. In particular, *Oishi Oitachi* is biographical prehistory and *Murakami Kiken* concerns a fictional character in later Gishiden storytelling. Kumoemon's death in 1916 is within the project's author/performer date threshold, but the improvised/performative status of Naniwabushi and any distinct text/arrangement credits need separate examination.

### Kumoemon's 1911 session / 1912 Lyrophone release — stronger discographic sub-lead

Further source triangulation has produced an exact, well-dated commercial group, although not yet an archival audio object:
- Takashi Otani's J-STAGE article says Tōchūken Kumoemon recorded four named performances in the Sanko-do studio in **December 1911**: **赤垣源蔵（徳利の別れ）** (*Akagaki Genzo: Tokkuri no Wakare*), **大石生立（御薬献上）** (*Oishi Oitachi: Goyaku Kenjō*), **村上喜剣** (*Murakami Kiken*), and **正宗孝子伝** (*Masamune Kōshiden*). Each performance occupied both sides of a disc. It says the copyright was registered on 28 February 1912 and transferred to Richard Werdermann.
- Yoshihisa Nomi's legal-history study provides a more detailed production and issue account: the recording engineer travelled from Germany; the masters were made in Tokyo, shipped to Germany, manufactured by Lyrophon and imported for Sanko-do distribution. It identifies five selections in the first release group, adding **南部坂後室の別れ / 南部坂後室雪の別れ** (*Nambuzaka Goshitsu no Wakare / Yuki no Wakare*) to the list above. The first group was advertised for sale in May 1912 (newspaper advertisements dated 18 and 31 May); the source describes a second group of five released on 18 November 1912. The first five discs cost 3 yen 80 sen each. The precise relationship between the J-STAGE four-title account and Nomi's five-title account is unresolved; preserve this discrepancy rather than silently reconciling it.
- The 1912 first-group label evidence reproduced by Nomi includes **LYROPHONE-RECORD / STARKTON** and a Japanese title label for *Murakami Kiken*; the paper reports the five original titles and a photographed label. This is stronger than a modern reissue discography for issue identity.
- A 2017 Osaka Kyoiku University study analyses the text/performance structure of Kumoemon's **南部坂後室 雪の別れ**, gives an opening lyric excerpt and identifies its source as a **1912 Lyrophone** record. The episode concerns Ōishi's visit to the widow/household of Asano before the attack, not the raid itself. It is a pre-revenge farewell / planning episode within the Gishi story-cycle; classification should remain **represented revenge context / collective grievance**, not automatically a standalone revenge lyric, until the full text is examined.
- A 2017 study of the **赤垣源蔵 徳利の別れ** recording lineage identifies Kumoemon's Lyrophone E1009 (1912), alongside later recordings. CiNii lists the Parlophone issue E1009 (70937–70938) and E1010 (70939–70940), but that catalogue entry itself gives no issue year; use the 1912 newspaper/production evidence, not the Parlophone reissue metadata, for dating the original.
- Kumoemon died in 1916. This satisfies the performer/reciter death-date threshold only if the project's composition rule treats the performed narration as his authored/improvised work; the Nomi paper describes the recording rights dispute as concerning his particular narrative performance, and historical Japanese legal analysis debates whether the improvised *naniwabushi* qualified as a stable musical work. Do not presume traditional provenance or authorship without identifying the text/reciter credit for each episode.

- Performer: 桃中軒雲右衛門入道, Kumoemonnyūdō Tōchūken.
- Label: Lyrophone Record.
- Record numbers: **70937–70940**, a two-disc set / four sides.
- Release month: **May 1912**.
- Archive object ID: **519319**.
- The entry says the original record was digitised with a laser turntable, phono equalizer and analogue-to-digital converter; its access status is internet-open because the archive publishes recordings whose copyright term has expired.
- A companion Kumoemon *Akagaki Genzo (Kinshu no kudari)* is archive ID **519351**, Lyrophone 70920–70921, November 1912.

The metadata search result is accessible, but direct opening of the record page returned HTTP 403 in this research session. Thus we have verified the archive's description of the digital transfer and public-access status, but have **not personally listened to the file or inspected the disc images**. Do not describe this as an audio-content verification.

The J-STAGE article gives a somewhat different first-session list: Kumoemon recorded four narratives at Sanko-do in December 1911, each across both sides of a disc; the list includes *Akagaki Genzo (Tokkuri no Wakare)*, *Oishi Oitachi (Goyaku Kenjō)*, *Murakami Kiken* and *Masamune Kōshiden*. It says Kumoemon registered the performance copyright and transferred it to Richard Werdermann on 28 February 1912. The later Nichibunken archive entry provides the exact label number/release date for *Akagaki Genzo*. The court case is especially important for the project's composition gate: the Supreme Court ultimately held that the recorded Naniwabushi was an improvised performance and not a copyrightable literary work under the law then in force; the 2013 J-STAGE author nevertheless argues that by modern criteria the performer could be considered an author because Naniwabushi performers shaped lyrics, dialogue and melody within a known plot. Keep historical legal doctrine distinct from present-day editorial assessment of authorship.

### B. Yoshida Naramaru, *Gishi no Uchiiri / Gishi no Hikiage* — stronger thematic lead, 1923

A specialist Japanese Victor/Nipponophone catalogue list identifies a two-sided 78rpm issue:
- **義士の討入** (*Gishi no Uchiiri*, “The Gishi raid”) — Yoshida Naramaru, Nipponophone **15139-A**.
- **義士の引揚** (*Gishi no Hikiage*, “The Gishi withdrawal/return”) — Yoshida Naramaru, Nipponophone **15139-B**.
- New-issue month: **July 1923**.
- Genre: Naniwabushi.
This is the most directly revenge-centred Japanese title surfaced so far: the A-side explicitly narrates the raid on Kira's residence, the retaliatory act at the centre of the Akō vendetta. The B-side follows the withdrawal/return. A separate historical-label catalogue also lists the pair under Yoshida Naramaru.

A pre-existing textual witness strengthens the repertoire context: the National Diet Library's 1912 *Gishi no Hitofushi: Naniwabushi Jūhachiban* (吉田奈良丸講演; 2nd Yoshida Naramaru, 1883–1967) contains a contents entry **義士吉良邸討入** (“Gishi raid on Kira's mansion”) and **義士の本懐を遂する條** (“the episode in which the Gishi fulfil their purpose”). The scanned text is from the 1912 *Hana no maki / Yuki no maki* volumes. This is a dated printed witness to the same traditional narrative episode, but it does not prove word-for-word identity with the 1923 recording. Yoshida Naramaru (2nd) died in 1967, so performer death alone does not satisfy the author's-death cutoff. The eligibility argument must rest on the **traditional narrative/performance repertoire with provenance**, not on an unsupported claim that the performer died before 1956.

The underlying Akō incident (1701–03) is historical, but the familiar forty-seven-rōnin story was extensively shaped by theatre, kōdan and Naniwabushi. Treat the recording as a **traditional narrative representation of collective revenge**, not an unmediated historical transcript. The 1912 printed contents establish the raid episode's place in Naramaru's repertoire; exact recorded lyrics and the degree of traditional versus performer-specific authorship remain open.

### C. Earlier Yamato On-ei recording evidence — by June 1914

A significant earlier source has surfaced in Hidenori Onishi's 2023 Ritsumeikan University study of the Kyoto record company Tōyō Chikuonki / Orient Record. The article reproduces a newspaper report from **Osaka Mainichi, Kyoto-Shiga supplement, 19 June 1914**. The report says Tokyo's **Yamato On-ei** company had commissioned Yoshida Naramaru to record and was selling under its **馬印 (horse-mark) label** the Naniwabushi selections **義士の討入** (*Gishi no Uchiiri*), **源蔵徳利の別れ** (*Genzo Tokkuri no Wakare*) and **南部坂** (*Nanbuzaka*), described as his signature pieces. The same report says a Kyoto company copied these records and sold the copies under a rival mark, prompting a trademark investigation. The article reproduces a photograph of the original Yamato On-ei Yoshida Naramaru record label (Figure 5).

This establishes that a commercial **Yoshida Naramaru recording titled Gishi no Uchiiri existed and was being sold no later than 19 June 1914**—earlier than the 1923 Nipponophone 15139-A listing. It is not yet enough to date the exact fixation or identify its matrix/catalogue number; it may be the original recording later re-recorded or reissued by Nipponophone, but that relationship must not be assumed. The 1914 newspaper account is contemporaneous evidence of sale, while Onishi's 2023 paper is the accessible reproduction and contextual source.

The 1912 printed *Gishi no Hitofushi: Naniwabushi Jūhachiban* by Yoshida Naramaru (2nd) contains a table-of-contents entry for **義士吉良邸討入** and a separate episode **義士の本懐を遂する條**. Its colophon gives printing on 1 March and publication on 5 March 1912. This makes a pre-1926 textual witness for Naramaru's raid episode available two years before the 1914 commercial recording report. It is a repertoire witness, not yet a word-for-word transcript of the recorded side.

The 1914 report names only “Yoshida Naramaru.” Given the active recording career of the second Naramaru (1879–1967), he is the probable performer, but the label photograph and original disc should be checked before making the identification definitive. Do not confuse him with the first Yoshida Naramaru (1851–1915), who had retired from the name in 1902. The 1912 book's NDL/Wikimedia metadata explicitly identifies the second Naramaru (1883–1967 in the NDL authority record; biographical sources often give 1879), so date of birth variants need a note, but all sources place his death in 1967.

Decision refinement: **Gishi no Uchiiri** is now a stronger high-priority lead with contemporaneous evidence of commercial sale by June 1914, a pictured Yamato On-ei label, and a 1912 printed Naramaru repertoire witness. ### D. Takamine Chikufu, *Gishi no Uchiiri* — 1924 Nipponophone and undated Menophone holdings

A second early recorded treatment of the raid appears in the specialist Nipponophone catalogue:
- **義士討入（一）（二）**, then **義士討入（三）（四）**, Takamine Chikufu (高峰筑風), label Nipponophone, catalogue numbers **15226–15227**, new-issue date **January 1924**, genre **Takamine biwa** (高峰琵琶). This is a four-side performance, not the same recording as Yoshida Naramaru's Naniwabushi side 15139-A.
- Takamine Chikufu (Suzuki Tetsurō, 1879–1936) founded the Takamine biwa school in 1912 and was a recognized biwa performer/composer. His death is before 1956, but the catalogue does not credit him as lyricist/composer for this particular *Gishi no Uchiiri*. Do not assume his performer credit automatically satisfies the author/composer rule.
- A 1981 Kushiro Municipal Museum catalogue of the Tokuji Sakai collection independently lists a physical four-side **義士の討入（一）〜（四）** by Takamine Chikufu on **Menophone 1618–1621**. This may be a different earlier issue or another label/version, but the museum catalogue gives no publication date and no matrix or transfer information. It is a real collection-level object, not proof of pre-1926 publication. The same collection lists other Akō-repertoire discs, including **続義士の本懐** (Toyoda Kyokusuì, Nipponophone 379–380) and **義士の本懐** (Toyoda Kyokusuì, Nipponophone 1799–1800), also without dates in this inventory.
- A separate 2020 study of modern biwa SP releases provides a broad catalogue framework and describes the growth of modern biwa schools and repertoire. It notes that the record market drew on historically popular narrative subjects, but the surfaced passages do not assign authorship of the 1924 *Gishi no Uchiiri* to Takamine.

### E. Additional 1912 printed witnesses for Naramaru's raid repertoire

Two separate 1912 printed sources now place the Kira-residence raid within Yoshida Naramaru's pre-recording repertoire:
1. *Gishi no Hitofushi: Naniwabushi Jūhachiban* (吉田奈良丸講演; Naramaru 2nd, NDL authority 1883–1967; published March 1912) lists **義士吉良邸討入** and **義士の本懐を遂する條** among the episodes in its *Hana no maki* contents. This is the Naramaru-authored/recited repertory witness discussed above.
2. Takuma Sadao's *Naniwabushi Hitofushi Zenshū* (浪花節一ト節全集), published May 1912, includes a section titled **討入** on p.39. Its contents also include Naramaru's *殿中の刃傷*, *大高源吾*, *赤垣源蔵*, *大石山科帰り*, *神崎与五郎* and *南部坂雪の別れ*. The anthology contains performances from several reciters (including Tōchūken Kumoemon and Azumaya Rakuyū), so the bare contents entry “討入” should not automatically be attributed to Naramaru until the actual page is checked. It nevertheless establishes that the raid story was circulating in the commercial Naniwabushi print/repertoire environment by May 1912.

These sources make the **traditional-repertoire provenance** argument stronger: the Chūshingura / Akō Gishi story cycle was not invented for a 1923 issue, but was part of an established Naniwabushi repertory documented in print before the 1914 Yamato recording report. Still, the exact 1914 disc's text must be matched to the printed version, and the narrative's traditional status should not erase the performer-specific shaping of the performance.

### F. Wider discography and genre chronology: the Gishi cycle on records

A useful scholarly control now establishes that the Akō Gishi revenge narrative entered the commercial phonograph medium earlier than the specific Naramaru Yamato item documented above:
- Henry D. Smith, “The Media and Politics of Japanese Popular History: The Case of the Akō Gishi” (2006), states that **the first record of a Gishi tale was issued in August 1910**, with many others following over the next several years. He notes the physical constraint of roughly three minutes per side, which meant that recorded Naniwabushi often captured only a segment of a much longer narrative, presuming listeners already knew the story.
- This is a scholarly chronology, not yet an identification of the August 1910 record's title, performer, label, catalogue number or matrix. Do not attribute it to Naramaru or Kumoemon without the cited primary discographical evidence.
- The same study describes the Gishi boom as drawing on the flourishing kōdan–Naniwabushi storytelling lineage and stresses the political ambiguity of the story's modern uses. That is important editorial context: the performance tradition was not a neutral transcript of the 1701–03 event, and the later “loyalty” reading should not erase its revenge plot or its different political interpretations.
- Alison Tokita's study of Naniwabushi genre classification describes Gishi-den as a kōdan-derived series whose content type is revenge (*ada-uchi*), with loyalty and pathos among its themes. She notes that Naniwabushi drew heavily on kōdan narrative material, literary adaptations and scripts, and that Kumoemon (1873–1916) helped transform the genre into a stage format with moral and patriotic messaging. This supports the genre-level classification, but does not prove that every specific disc is a traditional composition under the project's author/provenance gate.
- A 78MUSIC listing documents a distinct **Yoshida Naramaru, Nipponophone 15139-A/B**, dated July 1923: **義士の討入** (A) and **義士の引揚** (B). It is a firm pre-1926 issue lead, but it is not the same as the 1914 Yamato On-ei disc unless master comparison proves otherwise.
- A current second-hand listing describes a prewar Victor 52486 two-part **義士の討入 上下** by third Yoshida Naramaru. This is only a discovery lead: seller metadata is not reliable date or matrix evidence, and the issue may be later than the cutoff. Do not admit it as a candidate until an authoritative catalogue or physical label is checked.

Sources:
- Henry D. Smith, “The Media and Politics of Japanese Popular History: The Case of the Akō Gishi,” pp. 85–86: https://www.columbia.edu/~hds2/pdf/2006_Ako_Gishi_media_and_politics.pdf
- Alison Tokita, “The narrative worlds of contemporary *naniwa-bushi* (*rōkyoku*),” especially pp. 19–20: https://rijtm.kcua.ac.jp/pub/document2018-/publications/bulletin/15/kiyou15_tokita.pdf
- 78MUSIC Nipponophone issue listing, 15139-A/B, July 1923: https://www.78music.jp/other5.html
- Secondary-market listing for Victor 52486 (discovery only): https://auctions.yahoo.co.jp/search/search/78%E5%9B%9E%E8%BB%A2sp%E3%83%AC%E3%82%B3%E3%83%BC%E3%83%89/0/

Decision: this strengthens the Japanese Gishi/Naniwabushi research strand as a pre-1926 commercial recording ecosystem, not yet a cleared master. The 1910 first-issue identity remains unknown; the 1914 Yamato Naramaru disc remains a high-priority lead; the 1923 Nipponophone 15139-A/B is a separate documented issue; Victor 52486 remains unverified.


### G. The likely 1910 first Gishi record: Naramaru II's *Akō Gishiden* (provisional identification)

A further cross-check makes the 1910 first-record chronology more concrete, while leaving the exact disc identity open:
- Henry D. Smith dates the first commercial record of a Gishi tale to **August 1910**, without naming its performer/title/catalogue number in the passage.
- A Japanese academic article on the development of the content industry states that a Naniwabushi recording titled **赤穂義士伝 (*Akō Gishiden*)** appeared in **1910 (Meiji 43)** and had sold more than 200,000 copies by 1915. It cites Kurata Hiroyuki (2007), pp.65–70, as its source for the sales figure.
- A local-history article on Otaru's performance venues attributes the 1910 *Akō Gishiden* recording to **Yoshida Naramaru II** and repeats the 200,000-copy figure. This is a useful performer attribution, but should be corroborated against Kurata's book or a contemporary catalogue/advertisement before it is treated as definitive.
- The National Theatre's Cultural Digital Library independently documents the printed Naramaru performance text **『大和桜義士の面影』第一編**, dated **Meiji 43 (1910)**, and identifies it as a Naniwabushi performance by Yoshida Naramaru. The page explains that Naniwabushi drew much of its repertoire from kōdan, and that Gishi-den works frequently focus on individual tales and side stories rather than simply retelling the main event. This printed title is not necessarily identical to the 1910 disc's title or contents.
- A Japanese reference entry on Naniwabushi separately says that Tōchūken Kumoemon's **義士伝** record was released in **Taishō 1 (1912)**, reportedly pressing 72,000 copies at 3 yen 80 sen. This appears to be a distinct later Gishiden issue, not the 1910 first record. Keep it separate from the Nichibunken 1912 Lyrophone *Akagaki Genzo: Tokkuri no Wakare* (a specific farewell episode) and from Naramaru's 1914 Yamato and 1923 Nipponophone *Gishi no Uchiiri* issues.

Working conclusion: the **August 1910 first Gishi record may well be Yoshida Naramaru II's *Akō Gishiden***, but the identification is not yet secure enough for a candidate record. Obtain Kurata Hiroyuki, *Nihon rekōdo bunka-shi* (2007), pp.65–70, and locate the original 1910 Naramaru issue/catalogue/label before upgrading. The 1910 printed *Yamato Sakura Gishi no Omokage* is a separate textual witness and may help identify the exact recorded segment if matched.

### H. Nipponophone / early Japanese discography: exact Naramaru issue numbers and a primary-source verification route

A specialist combined Nitchiku-family discography (covering Nipponophone, Royal and American labels) now supplies a more concrete set of Naramaru Gishi-series catalogue numbers, while the National Diet Library (NDL) identifies the primary printed sources needed to date and transcribe them. The 1981 Kushiro museum inventory shows that no. 1645 was a **Royal** one-sided label, not the later Nipponophone eagle label. NDL's label chronology distinguishes the one-sided pre-1915 labels (including Royal) from the double-sided Nipponophone eagle label adopted from 1915:
- 78MUSIC lists the pre-1915 single-sided Naramaru series under Nipponophone / earlier eagle-face label variants:
  - **1645** — *赤穂義士討入* (*Akō Gishi Uchiiri*), Yoshida Naramaru.
  - **1646–1648** — *大高源吾* (parts 1–3).
  - **1649–1650** — *赤垣源蔵* (parts 1–2).
  - **1651** — *赤穂義士伝 殿中刃傷* (the palace-corridor assault / grievance precipitating the revenge), Naramaru.
  - **1652** — *義経安宅落* (not Gishi material).
  - **1653–1654** — *南部坂雪の別れ* (parts 1–2), Naramaru.
  - **1723–1724** — *赤穂浪士伝 間重次郎* (parts 1–2), Naramaru.
  These entries are not individually dated on the 78MUSIC page. Do not assign an issue year based only on their sequence or label family.
- The NDL's official prewar sales-catalogue guide records a **December 1912** *Shin-onpu tsuika mokuroku* (新音譜追加目録, YM2-L144), covering the Nipponophone eagle and red-paper labels; it also records March, April, May and November 1913 additions. This is a promising primary source to check whether Nos. 1645–1654 were already on sale by late 1912/1913. The catalogue is listed in the NDL holdings guide as a paper item, not as an openly digitized PDF.
- NDL's Nitchiku reference guide explains that a **1914 fifth-edition *Chikuonki monku-shū*** (蓄音器文句集, 1914, call no. KH13-L245) transcribes Nitchiku record contents through January 1914, including title, performer, issue number and the actual spoken/sung text. If Nos. 1645/1651/1653 are included, this could give us a primary textual witness for the recorded Naramaru repertoire.
- The same NDL guide identifies Kurata Hiroyuki and National Theatre's *Engei Record Sales Catalogue* (1990, call no. KD1-E21), covering Japanese performance records from 1909–1945 and listing title, performer, author, company, catalogue number and issue month in release order. This is the strongest next reference to consult for the 1910 *Akō Gishiden* and the early Naramaru issues.
- The Kyoto City University of Arts archive history of Nitchiku confirms the company began its own disc manufacture in 1907 under the Nitchiku predecessor, that Nitchiku established its corporate identity in 1910, and that it recalled its old single-sided labels in 1915 when changing to double-sided Nipponophone eagle labels. Therefore the early 1645–1654 Naramaru titles are in the single-sided-era catalogue, not automatically the later 1923 double-sided 15139 issue.

### I. Contemporary 1911 Nipponophone advertisement: ten Naramaru Gishi recordings

A contemporary newspaper advertisement, transcribed by a Japanese record-history researcher from **Asahi Shimbun, 4 September 1911 (Meiji 44)**, materially strengthens the chronology:
- The advertisement says Nipponophone had produced ten new records recorded by the leading Naniwabushi performer **Yoshida Naramaru**, to be released from **1 September 1911**.
- It lists (i) **赤穂義士 傳間重次郎** (*Akō Gishi-den: Hazama Jūjirō*), parts 上・下; (ii) **同 神崎興五郎** (*Kanzaki Yogorō*), parts 一〜四; (iii) **同 赤垣源藏** (*Akagaki Genzo*), parts 一・二; and (iv) **同 大石内藏助** (*Ōishi Kuranosuke*), parts 上・下. This is a ten-side Gishi cycle: 2 + 4 + 2 + 2.
- This is direct contemporary evidence for the commercial release of a substantial Naramaru Gishi repertoire by September 1911, distinct from the later 1914 Yamato On-ei *Gishi no Uchiiri* item. It does **not** identify the 1911 sides as the actual raid episode; the listed titles concern Hazama Jūjirō, Kanzaki Yogorō, Akagaki Genzo and Ōishi Kuranosuke.
- The same researcher transcribes an **Asahi Shimbun, 5 April 1913** Nipponophone advertisement listing Naramaru's *Asano Takumi no Kami saigo* (浅野内匠頭最後), parts 1–4, as new releases. This is the precipitating grievance in the Chūshingura cycle, not the revenge action itself.
- CiNii / International Research Center for Japanese Studies catalogue records independently describe a physical Naramaru SP **Hazama Jūjirō: Akō Gishi-den**, Nipponophone/Royal Record 1723, and a broader Nipponophone SP series catalogued as *Akō Gishi-den* with Naramaru performer credit and individual episodes including *Ōishi Kuranosuke*, *Kanzaki Yogorō*, *Hazama Jūjirō*, *Nanbuzaka yuki no wakare*, *Tono-naka jinshō*, *Akagaki Genzo* and *Ōtaka Gen'go*. These catalogue records do not supply publication dates; the 1911 ad makes a plausible connection for the Hazama title, but the number 1723 should be matched to the original disc before asserting it is one of the advertised 1911 issues.
- NDL's current guide to prewar sales catalogues confirms that it holds the December 1912 Nipponophone *Shin-onpu tsuika mokuroku* (YM2-L144) and 1913 additions (YM2-M2340–2344). It describes monthly sales catalogues as listings of new releases and warns that annual/catalogue issue dates may postdate the actual record release. The NDL catalogue inventory does not itself reveal the listed song titles; the paper items must be inspected.
- A 1914 second edition of *Nihon Chikuonki Monku Zenshū* (日本蓄音器文句全集), edited by Itō Naoki and published February 1914, is catalogued at Tokyo Gakugei University. It is a substantial 690-page text collection and a possible primary route to exact recorded wording. Do not assume it is the same item as the separately cited 1914 *Chikuonki monku-shū* without bibliographic collation.

## Additional institutional confirmation of a Naramaru Royal *Gishi-den* disc

Two further institutional sources now corroborate the existence and repertoire context of Yoshida Naramaru's early *Gishi* recordings, without yet fixing the release date or proving that the physical Royal disc is catalogue no.1645.

1. The International Research Center for Japanese Studies (Nichibunken), in its 2016 prospectus section on the interdisciplinary study of Naniwabushi, reproduces a photograph/caption for an SP record: **Yoshida Naramaru, “Akō Gishiden Toiri” (赤穂義士伝討入), Royal Record**, held by Nichibunken. This is an institutional collection reference to a physical disc, independent of the 78MUSIC discography and the Kushiro inventory. The prospectus does not give a catalogue number, matrix, date or audio-transfer details, so it cannot yet be equated with Kushiro's Royal 1645.
2. The Japan Arts Council's Cultural Digital Library identifies a **Naramaru performance handbill for “Gishi Uchiiri” (義士討入)** in the National Theatre collection and documents *Yamato Sakura Gishi no Omokage*, first part, as a 1910 Naramaru performance text held by the National Diet Library. It describes Naniwabushi as a performance art in which a narrator conveys stories through melodic vocal passages and dialogue with shamisen accompaniment. It also notes that the *Akō Gishi-den* repertoire includes many *meimei-den* (individual biographies) and *gaiden* (supplementary tales), often emphasizing the emotional conflict between **giri** (duty/obligation) and **ninjō** (human feeling) rather than simply the plot's action.
3. This institutional framing strengthens the case that Naramaru's *Gishi* repertoire was commercially and textually established before the 1914 Yamato On-ei report, but it also cautions against flattening the whole repertoire into a single “revenge song.” The *Uchiiri* title explicitly identifies the raid episode; individual Naniwabushi pieces may foreground loyalty, obligation, separation, sacrifice or emotional conflict. The exact 1645 / Nichibunken / 1914 Yamato texts and masters still need comparison.

**Status update:** physical-disc and repertoire evidence is now stronger; exact issue chronology, master identity, text, transfer and rights remain unresolved. Keep the Naramaru Royal disc as a **HIGH-PRIORITY PHYSICAL LEAD / NOT YET CANDIDATE / NOT CLEARED**.

### I. Royal 1651: independent label chronology and sequence corroboration (2026-11-15)

The 78MUSIC record-level table explicitly places **1651** under “Royal Record — red-label eagle mark” (ローヤル - 赤レーベル鷲印), with entry “赤穂義士伝 殿中刃傷,” Osaka Yoshida Naramaru, one-sided, front-facing eagle. This is more precise than merely inferring a Royal-family association from the issue number. The same table separates earlier Royal lion-mark sections and later Nipponophone 15000 series. See https://www.78music.jp/other5.html, especially the section headings and rows 92–99 at https://www.78music.jp/other5.html (page source also accessible as https://www.78music.jp/other5.html).

Independent chronology support: the National Diet Library's prewar record-label reference says that through 1915 the one-sided labels included Symphony, Royal, Globe, American and Universal; from 1915 onward the company shifted to double-sided Nipponophone. It further explains the 1912 label/mark distinctions, including red-paper Royal = red eagle mark. https://crd.ndl.go.jp/reference/entry/index.php?id=2000030112&page=man_view . 78MUSIC's own label history says Royal began in the late Meiji period, was a one-sided ¥1.25 issue, moved to double-sided records in the Taishō period and was discontinued in Taishō 4 (1915); the Royal eagle design is described as an early mark. https://www.78music.jp/hNipponophone.html .

The sequence is now corroborated more tightly: 1645 *Akō Gishi Uchiiri*; 1646–48 *Ōtaka Gengō*; 1649–50 *Akagaki Genzō*; **1651 *Akō Gishiden: Denchū Ninjō***; 1652 *Yoshitsune Ataka-ochi*; 1653 *Nanbuzaka Yuki no Wakare* upper part. The 78MUSIC listing separately gives 1651's one-sided/front-facing eagle note. This confirms the catalogue identification and the label classification at the level of the specialist catalogue, but not the actual physical specimen. 78MUSIC explains that its catalogue is compiled from company new-release lists, newspaper advertisements, discographies and biographies, and that issue month is normally treated as release month; where no month is shown, no exact release date can be assigned. https://78music.jp/lisitTop.html .

**Reassessment:** B — PROBABLE chronology remains justified, now with stronger independent support for the pre-1915 Royal label chronology. It is still conditional on the surviving disc actually bearing the red-label eagle Royal mark described by the catalogue. Do not state a precise year. A physical label photograph or archival inspection is still needed to verify the catalogue's classification against the disc in hand; matrix/runout and audio/text remain uninspected. The 1912 Naramaru printed text establishes the repertoire and thematic fit, not the recording date or exact recorded wording.

A useful negative control: 78MUSIC lists the later three-disc Taihei *Denchū Ninjō* as 1935, and CiNii/Nichibunken catalogues the separate four-disc Taihei set (Naramaru III) only as [19--]. Neither is the 1651 Royal item. https://www.78music.jp/taihei.html ; https://ci.nii.ac.jp/ncid/BB17753999 .


### J. Physical-collection inventory corroborates Royal 1651 (Kushiro Municipal Museum, 1981)

A much stronger independent check has surfaced. Hirotsugu Satō's “Notes on the Collection of Records in Kushiro Municipal Museum II” (Kushiro Municipal Museum Bulletin no. 8, 1981), inventory section, states that 1,347 records were transferred from Kushiro Municipal Library to the museum in April 1979 and then catalogued. In the Yoshida Naramaru entry sequence, the inventory lists:
- no. 1645, *Akō Gishi Uchiiri*;
- no. 1653, *Akō Gishiden*;
- no. **1651, *Akō Gishiden, Denchū Ninjō*, one-sided, Royal**;
- no. 1723–24, *Akō Gishiden, Hazama Jūjirō*;
- no. 1650, *Akagaki Genzō*;
- no. 1646 and 1648, *Akō Gishiden / Ōtaka Gengō*;
- a second table separately records 1649 and 1653–54 as Royal.
The table's performer cell uses ditto marks following the explicit “Yoshida Naramaru” row, so attribution is inherited from that row. The 1651 item is thus not merely a modern discographical reconstruction: a physical example was recorded in a municipal museum collection inventory by 1981.
