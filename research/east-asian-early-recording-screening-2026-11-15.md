# East Asian early-recording screening — Korea and Cantonese/Chinese records (2026-11-15)

Status: **regional screening / no new candidate admitted**.

## Korea: Fletcher cylinders (1896) and the disputed 1899 recording event

The strongest securely documented early Korean audio object surfaced in this pass is the Alice Cunningham Fletcher collection: six Edison wax cylinders recorded in Washington, D.C., on 24 July 1896, with Korean students Ahn Jeong-sik, Lee Hee-Cheol and Son Rong. The Korean History Net account describes roughly 17 minutes of recordings and lists “Maehwa taryeong,” an “Aegukga” in folk style, “Arirang” (Haeju Arong taryeong), “Jebiga,” “Dala dala,” and a hand-clapping game. The Library of Congress confirms Fletcher's date and performers and describes these as early ethnographic recordings. They are **field/academic recordings**, not commercial releases. None of the song titles or catalogue descriptions presently establishes revenge, spite or retaliation; the patriotic and folk materials should not be classified by title alone.

A discrepancy emerged around the often-repeated 1899 Gamunjeong Pavilion event in Seoul. The National Gugak Center's online exhibition says government officials recorded *Chunhyang-ga*, gasa and japga there in 1899. However, the Korean History Net account states that no surviving Korean cylinder recordings from the period had yet been found, and identifies the six 1896 Fletcher cylinders as the oldest located recordings. Until an exact surviving 1899 cylinder, inventory or archival audio object is identified, the 1899 event is **historical recording testimony, not a verified extant master**.

*Chunhyang-ga* may warrant a textual search because its narrative includes the corrupt magistrate Byeon Hak-do's abuse of Chunhyang and his eventual punishment when Yi Mong-ryong returns in an official capacity. That is a possible **punishment / justice** mechanism in a literary narrative, not automatically revenge; moreover, the claimed 1899 recording is not yet an identified surviving audio object. The 1937 Okeh *Chunhyangjeon* set is far outside the cutoff and cannot stand in for the 1899 event.

## Cantonese / Chinese recording infrastructure and item leads

The Chinese Historical Society of Southern California's Phonograph Record Collection contains about 250 records, mostly Cantonese (Yueju) opera on 10-inch shellac discs, produced in the first half of the 20th century in China, England, Germany and the United States. The finding aid includes at least one dated early item: Victor Talking Machine Co. 1769, catalogued as **入仙賀寿**, Camden, New Jersey, 1902, a 10-inch 78rpm disc sent for digitization. The item has a digital-material link, but its performer, dialect, matrix, precise recording date, lyrics and relevance to grievance are not established in the finding-aid entry. Treat it as an archival object to inspect, not as a candidate.

A separate title-level lead in the same collection is **梨娘恨** (catalogued with performer Bao Yu / 寶玉; Golden Star G-S 39 A/B and G-S 39 C/D). The title contains 恨 (“hatred/resentment”), but the record is undated and the lyric/plot is unknown. This is a **title lead only**, not evidence of a grudge or revenge song. Another Golden Star item, **滿天神佛**, is also undated and not grievance evidence.

For the early commercial industry, Yung Sai-shing's study reports that the Gramophone Company and Edison began producing Cantonese songs, operatic songs, ballads and folk songs in 1903. The Gramophone Company Discography's 430,000-plus entry database is a key way to search its global matrices and issue numbers, but the underlying title/person fields are inconsistent and must be cross-checked against catalogues and labels. A specialist discographical summary describes Gaisberg's March–April 1903 Chinese expedition as 476 records: 329 Mandarin-dialect titles recorded in Shanghai and 147 Cantonese-dialect titles recorded in Hong Kong. This is a search map, not candidate evidence.

## Greek chronology caution

A Cambridge note on rebetiko chronology warns that dates assigned to pre-war recordings are often unreliable and not based on factual evidence. Any Greek/Ottoman urban song discovered through later rebetiko discographies must be checked against matrix chronology, original catalogues, labels and session documentation. Do not treat a modern discography's date as publication or fixation proof.

Source:
- Ole L. Smith, “The chronology of Rebetiko — a reconsideration of the evidence” (1991; online 2016): https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/byzantine-and-modern-greek-studies/article/abs/chronology-of-rebetiko-a-reconsideration-of-the-evidence/D5BFC4A14B02375A1ED58D5135839935

## Decisions and handoff

- No Korean, Chinese/Cantonese or Greek item is admitted as a candidate in this pass.
- The 1896 Fletcher cylinders are real, eligible by fixation date and traditional repertoire may meet composition criteria, but the surfaced tracks do not establish the project's grievance theme and their field-recording/rights status is separate from commercial records.
- The 1899 Korean *Chunhyang-ga* recording event is disputed at the level of surviving master identification; do not cite it as an extant audio object until an item is located.
- The Chinese Historical Society collection offers a productive archive path, but the 1902 Victor item needs listening and discographical identification; **梨娘恨** needs dating and lyric/context verification.
- Next: inspect the California Revealed digitization of Victor 1769; request the Golden Star discs' labels, matrixes and dates; search the 1903 G&T Hong Kong catalogue and 1904 Chinese catalogue by Chinese title and operatic plot; identify any *Chunhyang-ga* master actually surviving from the 1899 event; and check Fletcher-cylinder rights/terms before considering any use.


## Japan: Gishi / Chūshingura rōkyoku recordings — high-value new lead

The International Research Center for Japanese Studies (Nichibunken) maintains a dedicated **Rōkyoku (Naniwabushi) SP Record Digital Archive**. Its stated corpus is 9,998 SP records donated by collector Morikawa Tsukasa, published from the Meiji 30s through the Showa 30s; the interface includes record-surface images, audio and related materials. The archive says all disc images are open online, while audio is openly playable only where the copyright term has expired; other audio is available only on-site. It digitises playback using a laser turntable, phono equalizer and analogue-to-digital converter. This is an unusually strong research path because the archive exposes label/catalogue images and a direct searchable database, though the web search interface itself returned 403 in this session.

Henry D. Smith's study of the Akō Gishi states that the first Gishi tale record was issued in **August 1910**, with many more following in the next years. He explains that three-minute sides generally captured selected song/story segments, not a complete extended tale; a record title may therefore represent one episode within a larger revenge story. The Akō vendetta itself is a historical event (1701–03), while many later *Gishiden* episodes include fictional characters and accretions. Do not treat every episode as a transparent record of the historical event.

The same archive's search-indexed entries surfaced several later *Gishi* items, useful as proof that this corpus is searchable by episode title but **not yet pre-1926 candidates**:
- **(義士傳) 安兵衞變裝** (*Gishiden: Yasubee hensō*), Tōchūken Jōun, Regal / Columbia 65232–65233, issued January 1933; archive record 519216.
- **(義士外傳) 大竹重兵衛** (*Gishi gaiden: Ōtake Jūbei*), Kiyomi Tsuda, Augon A5178, issue date not supplied; archive record 517460.
- **義士引揚 / 義士最後** (*Gishi hikiage / Gishi saigo*), Yoshida Naramaru, Nipponophone 1891–1892; CiNii record BB17665563 gives no issue year, so it cannot be assumed eligible.
- **赤穂義士 神嵜與五郎 (一)/(二)**, Yoshida Naramaru, Parrot 412/413; a specialist Japanese label list gives no new-issue date. Lead only.
- A 78music label list also shows **義士討入** (*Gishi uchiiri*, “Gishi attack/raid”), Yoshida Naramaru, Special Record, and **殿中刃傷** (“wounding in the palace”), but without catalogue/date evidence.

A separate Japanese legal-history source identifies a particularly important 1912 commercial group by **Tōchūken Kumoemon (1873–1916)**: “Akagaki Genzo Tokkuri no Wakare,” “Nambuzaka Goshitsu Yuki no Wakare,” “Oishi Oitachi,” “Murakami Kiken,” and “Masamune Koshiden,” sold on 19 May 1912 after a high-profile exclusive recording contract. These titles belong to the Akō Gishi story-cycle, but none should be labelled a revenge song until the exact side's words/episode are established. In particular, *Oishi Oitachi* is biographical prehistory and *Murakami Kiken* concerns a fictional character in later Gishiden storytelling. Kumoemon's death in 1916 is within the project's author/performer date threshold, but the improvised/performative status of Naniwabushi and any distinct text/arrangement credits need separate examination.
