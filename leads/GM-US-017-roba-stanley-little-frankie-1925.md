# GM-US-017 — Roba Stanley & Bill Patterson, “Little Frankie” (Okeh 40436, matrix 8848; recorded December 1924)

**Status:** B — probable core candidate; exact issue/matrix identity and narrative are documented, but original issue date and master-to-surviving-transfer chain need final confirmation  
**Date assessment:** PROBABLE CORE DATE — matrix discography dates the session to December 1924; the 1925 Okeh 40436 issue is supported by specialist discography and archival finding-aid references  
**Proposed role:** revenge / retaliatory murder ballad, female-voiced Frankie variant  
**Recording:** Roba Stanley with Bill Patterson; Okeh 40436, “Little Frankie,” matrix 8848

## Exact recording identity

- Okeh 78rpm numerical discography lists **40436 — Stanley & Patterson — “Little Frankie” — matrix 8848 — 12/??/24**. The paired side is Roba Stanley's “Single Life,” matrix 9215, dated July 1925.
- The same discography lists Okeh 40436 under the 1925 issue sequence; the matrix/session date for “Little Frankie” is December 1924.
- The Library of Congress American Folklife Center's Pete Welding Collection finding aid includes “Little Frankie” by Roba Stanley and Bill Patterson, identifies Okeh 40436, and provides a short content note: “Frankie woke up one morning” and “he's my man but he done me wrong.” The log marks the sound quality poor. This is a later tape dub/catalogue entry, not the original 78 itself.
- A Charles Wolfe chapter on Roba Stanley's recordings describes her “Little Frankie” as a version of “Frankie and Johnnie”; it reports that the song narrates Frankie killing Alvin after he wronged her, followed by a trial and acquittal. The chapter gives additional verse content and treats the shift in gender perspective as significant.

## Thematic assessment

This is more than a title-only association. The LOC finding aid preserves the opening narrative and the lover's wrongdoing; Wolfe's account identifies the performance as a female-voiced version of “Frankie and Johnnie” in which Frankie kills Alvin, is tried and acquitted. The song therefore represents retaliation for perceived romantic betrayal, with a woman as the retaliating protagonist. It is a narrative representation, not evidence of the singer's own experience or endorsement.

Do not collapse the performance into a generic “murder ballad”: its gendered point of view and the shift from the more common male-centred versions are important. The complete lyrics of the Okeh master have not been transcribed in this dossier; the exact verse sequence and whether the recorded take matches the text discussed by Wolfe remain to be checked.
