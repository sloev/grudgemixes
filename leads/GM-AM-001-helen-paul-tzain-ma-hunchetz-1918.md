- Performer: Helen Paul (soprano, accompanied by violin); biographical identity remains unknown.

## Work, text and historical context

The museum identifies the song as an ode to the earliest organized Armenian revolutionary groups, especially the Protectors of the Fatherland (Bashdban Hayrenyat's), an organization associated with Erzerum. The composition is attributed to an unknown writer and is believed to date from the late nineteenth century.

The Armenian Museum links the song's history to the Nishkian brothers, founders of the Protectors of the Fatherland, whose families fled Erzerum and settled in Fresno, California. Kalem Hapet Nishkian later operated a phonograph shop in Fresno and carried the Columbia disc. This is an unusually direct chain connecting a revolutionary song, forced migration, diaspora commerce and the circulation of recorded sound.

The museum shares a 1924 printed score from *Songs of Armenia*, edited by C. H. Paelian and arranged by K. H. Aiqouni. This is a near-contemporary text witness, but it is not proof of the exact words or arrangement on Helen Paul's 1918 master. Obtain Armenian-language review and compare the score with the recording before quoting.

## Thematic assessment

The song is a historical ode to revolutionary organizers and can fit collective resistance / revolutionary memory. It may carry an underlying grievance against Ottoman repression, but the exact recorded text has not yet been transcribed or examined. Do not assign a specific retaliatory narrative, event or political factional programme without further evidence.

The 1918 recording predates the establishment of the independent Republic of Armenia on 28 May 1918 by several weeks, according to the museum's account. This chronological proximity is important, but do not frame the record as a state anthem: it is identified as an ode to earlier revolutionary groups.

The diaspora setting is not incidental. The recording was made in New York by an Armenian singer whose biography remains unknown, and the disc was sold through a Fresno shop operated by a family linked to the song's revolutionary history. Preserve this as a transnational Armenian/Ottoman history rather than treating it merely as an American ethnic recording.

## Research update — Helen Paul / “Tzain ma Hunchetz” (2026-11-17)

Rechecked the Armenian Museum of America's own Sound Archive article and archive overview. The museum directly identifies Helen Paul's April 1918 Columbia session in the Woolworth Building, the two recorded titles (“Mer Hairenik” and “Tzain ma Hunchetz”), the violin-accompanied soprano format, the ode's dedication to the Erzerum revolutionary groups and the late-19th-century composition attribution. It also confirms that the museum's roughly 3,000-disc collection is selectively digitised and shared. The 1924 Songs of Armenia score is a later text witness, not proof of the exact 1918 take.
