## Text and translation

The museum identifies the song in early twentieth-century Armenian songbooks as “Hayrenaser” (“Patriot”). It says Tashjian's Columbia version opens with lyrics that differ from variants printed in those songbooks. The museum publishes this transcription/translation, while noting that the physical disc's condition prevented a reliable transcription of the first verse.

Selected lines in the museum's English translation:

- “Forget your homeland, the foreigners say”
- “It’s cursed with only thorns and ruins”
- “Our land is eden, its waters are refreshing”
- “Come emigre, give your heart to it”
- “I [would still] love Hayastan, long live my dear Cilicia”
- “Let the universe forget the greatness of the Armenian”
- “I still love and won’t forget”

The translation is the museum's rendering. Obtain Armenian-language review before quoting in published liner notes; preserve the Armenian original and the uncertainty in the damaged opening verse.

## Historical and thematic assessment

The song stages a confrontation between the emigrant and an unnamed “foreigner” who urges abandonment of the homeland as barren and ruined. The singer's answer is emphatically defiant: he will continue to love and remember Armenia and Cilicia. This makes the recording a strong example of diasporic grievance and resistance to assimilation, although it is not a direct account of a single retaliatory act or a fully documented protest against a named policy.

The museum places the recording in the immediate aftermath of the April 1909 Adana massacres. It documents community protests in Boston in August 1909, including a resolution condemning the Ottoman government's response. The museum asks whether the recordings were made in this atmosphere and speculates that discs may have been used to raise humanitarian funds. Treat that fundraising purpose as a hypothesis, not an established fact.

The record's transnational circulation matters: Tashjian recorded in New York, and the museum traces the family's later record-dealing activity and Armenian community networks. The recording should be interpreted within Armenian diaspora life and Ottoman Armenian history, not reduced to a generic “ethnic” or American immigrant artifact.
