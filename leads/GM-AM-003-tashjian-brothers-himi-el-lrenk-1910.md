# GM-AM-003 — Tashjian Brothers, “Himi El Lrenk” (Columbia E668/4953, 1910)

**Region:** Armenian diaspora / New York; Armenian revolutionary-patriotic song tradition  
**Status:** B — exceptionally strong thematic text and exact Columbia issue identified; exact 1910 take still requires audio transcription  
**Proposed role:** Collective protest / anti-subjugation / refusal of silence / armed resistance rhetoric  
**Retrieval:** RESEARCH TARGET — Armenian Museum has original disc and archive selections; request item-level audio and rights

## Song text and independent text witnesses

The Armenian Museum identifies “Himi El Lrenk” as one of the 28 songs recorded by the Tashjian Brothers in 1910 and reproduces the Columbia E668/4953 label. The museum does not publish a full transcription of this particular 1910 performance.

The Armenian-language site ERGIR publishes a text under the title “ՀԻՄԻ ԷԼ ԼՌԵՆՔ” and attributes its words to a poem by Raphael Patkanian (1830–1892), “The Death of Vardan Mamikonian.” It describes the melody as circulating in folk variants. This is a song-family text witness, not proof that every stanza appears on the 1910 Columbia side:
https://ergir.am/himi_el_lrenq/

The published Armenian text repeatedly asks whether Armenians should remain silent while an enemy puts a sword to their chest, ignores their cries, takes their land and weapons, forges chains from sword and plough, drives them from their homeland, and attacks their church and cultural identity. Its final stanza rejects silence and calls the brave to confront the enemy, even at the cost of death rather than submission.

An English translation of a related text appears in *The Armenian Weekly* under the title “How Long Shall We Be Silent?” and is attributed to Raphael Patkanian:
https://tert.nla.am/archive/NLA%20TERT/Armenian%20weekly/2014/31_ocr.pdf

A further account on ERGIR interprets the poem as a call to resist enslavement, displacement and cultural destruction. Treat that as an interpretive source, not as a substitute for a historical-critical edition or the exact 1910 recorded lyric.

## Thematic assessment

This is an exceptionally strong song-family match for the project's core: collective grievance, accusation of oppression, forced displacement, chains, loss of arms and land, attacks on religious/cultural institutions, refusal of silence, and explicit call to resistance. It is not merely patriotic praise.

The poem's attribution to Raphael Patkanian places its literary origins in the nineteenth century. The recording by the Tashjian Brothers in New York in 1910 documents its circulation in Armenian diaspora musical culture before the Armenian Genocide. Later uses of the song in other political contexts demonstrate its afterlife, but must not be projected backward onto the 1910 performance.

The song's historical framing is Armenian national-patriotic and invokes an “enemy” in a specific collective historical narrative. Preserve the source's language and context; do not turn it into a generic universal anti-authoritarian anthem or assume that all Armenian communities interpreted it identically.

## Identity clarification — a separate 1909 solo recording

The Armenian Museum's expanded discography also identifies a 1909 solo Mardiros Der Sarkis Tashjian “Himi El Lrenk,” Columbia E529-4100, paired/listed with “Marseillaise.” This is distinct from the 1910 Tashjian Brothers “Himi El Lrenk,” Columbia E668/4953, documented in this dossier. The shared title does not make them the same performance or issue.
