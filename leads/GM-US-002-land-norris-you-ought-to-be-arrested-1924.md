# Lead: “You Ought to Be Arrested and Put in Jail” — Land Norris, Okeh 40374 (1924)

**Status: C — TITLE-LEVEL LEAD; NOT YET A THEMATIC CANDIDATE.** The physical recording, performer, issue and session are well identified; the song's actual words and dramatic situation have not yet been checked. Do not infer a serious justice demand from the title alone.

## Recording and issue evidence

- **Performer / title / issue:** Land Norris, “You Ought to Be Arrested and Put in Jail,” paired with “Fox Chase in Georgia,” Okeh 40374.
- **Carrier:** Smithsonian National Museum of American History describes a 10-inch shellac sound recording by Land Norris, manufacturer Okeh, recording date 1924, maker/catalogue number 40374; object ID 1996.0320.22094.
- **Session / matrix:** UCSB's Discography of American Historical Recordings (DAHR) Joe Bussard Collection list gives the title, Okeh 40374, matrix 8701, session 31 August 1924. A specialist sessionography gives 26 August 1924 and matrix 8701-A. Preserve the five-day discrepancy; do not silently select one date. DAHR matrix page: https://adp.library.ucsb.edu/index.php/matrix/detail/2000200840 .
- **Contemporary issue evidence:** *The Talking Machine World*, 15 June 1925, p. 167, lists Okeh 40374 with both titles under “Old-Time Tune Records,” described as singing with banjo accompaniment by Land Norris. This establishes commercial listing by June 1925.
- **Audio:** DAHR's Joe Bussard Collection page explicitly lists this track among recordings digitized by the Dust-to-Digital Foundation and made available online through DAHR. Following the collection-page link resolves to the correct DAHR matrix record **2000200840** (not 2000200717; the latter URL in the earlier dossier was incorrect). The DAHR record itself returned HTTP 403 in this research pass, so playback, download, transfer details and rights terms could not be inspected. No audio audition or transfer comparison was completed. Smithsonian's physical-object record is not itself a listening copy.


## Newly identified song-family context (not yet linked to the exact master)

A useful lead emerged from comparing the title with early song-family references:

- A specialist folk-song blog groups the Land Norris 1924 Okeh 40374 recording under the wider **“Poor Mourner” / “Mourner, You Shall Be Free” / “You Shall Be Free”** family, alongside the 1896 Standard Quartette, 1898 Cousins and DeMoss, 1902 Dinwiddie Colored Quartet, 1911 Fisk Jubilee Quartet and later Uncle Dave Macon recordings. It also reproduces E. C. Perrow's 1915 “Oh, Mourner!” text, whose 1909 Mississippi field-note source includes comic verses about stealing food, calling “police,” and avoiding work. This is a secondary song-family crosswalk, not a verified transcript or proof that every listed recording shares the same text.
- The same source places Land Norris immediately after the Fisk Jubilee Quartet entry, and before Uncle Dave Macon's 1926 “Shout Mourner You Shall Be Free.” A Mudcat discussion independently reports that Meade's *Country Music Sources* indexes Land Norris's title under “Mourner, You Shall Be Free,” but this is a forum report of a secondary reference, not direct evidence from Meade itself.
- The Ballad Index's “Talking Blues” entry describes a humorous, floating-verse song tradition and cross-references “Mourner, You Shall Be Free (Moanish Lady).” It separately cites Harbin's “Oughta Been Arrested” (part “Two Little Niggers Black as Tar,” part “Talking Blues,” part other material). This may explain the title/verse family but does **not** yet identify the exact Land Norris performance as that text.
- These sources make a humorous floating-verse / minstrel-derived song-family connection more plausible than a straightforward serious call for legal punishment. They do not establish the recorded lyric, and they raise a further contextual obligation: any future liner note must address the Black-origin spiritual/folk material, its later commercial/minstrel transformations, and racist caricature without treating those layers as interchangeable.

**Current interpretation:** still C — TITLE-LEVEL LEAD. The likely family context is now more informative, but there is no exact lyric-to-master link. Do not classify as punishment, accusation or grievance until the audio or an exact contemporaneous text is checked.

## Primary text comparison — Perrow's 1915 “Oh, Mourner!” (new research, 2026-11-15)

The full digitized text of E. C. Perrow's “Oh, Mourner!” is visible in a transcription of *Journal of American Folklore*, vol. 28, no. 108 (1915), “Songs and Rhymes from the South,” part VII, “Songs of the Plantation.” Perrow labels the item “From Mississippi; negroes; MS. of F. R. Rubel; 1909.” A note beneath the text says the ballad was obtained from a person named Rasmus Moore on 24 February 1909. The text is explicitly composite: Perrow's note says distinct songs tend to drift together into one.

The text includes the stanza: “I had a wife and I fed her on grease; / Every time I knocked her down she hollered ‘police!’” It also contains comic theft accusations, a spiritual refrain (“Oh, Moana, you shall be free”), and other floating verses. This is a primary historical text witness for a performance tradition that combines violence, police-calling, comic accusation and a spiritual refrain. It is **not** evidence that Land Norris sang this stanza, that the title “You Ought to Be Arrested and Put in Jail” derives from it, or that the 1924 Okeh performance has a serious punitive meaning.

A specialist secondary crosswalk places Norris's Okeh 40374 recording in the “Poor Mourner / Mourner, You Shall Be Free” family. That crosswalk is plausible but unconfirmed at the master/lyric level. Ballad Index independently cites Harbin's “Oughta Been Arrested” as a composite of “Two Little Niggers Black as Tar,” “Talking Blues” and other material, but does not itself prove a connection to Norris. The shared arrest language and related song-family labels are a research lead, not an identity match.

**Thematic assessment unchanged: C — TITLE-LEVEL LEAD.** The Perrow text demonstrates that the wider tradition can include a woman calling police after violence, but the stanza is voiced through a racist minstrel-derived comic frame and does not straightforwardly amount to a victim-centred protest song. Any use would require careful attribution, historical contextualisation and attention to the racist language and performance economy. Do not classify this as revenge or justice-demand content without the actual Okeh audio/text.
