# GM-ZA-001 — Sol T. Plaatje, “Nkosi Sikelel’ iAfrika” (Zonophone 4168, matrix Bb3640-2, 1923)

## Status

**Grade B — strong anticolonial collective-protest context; exact recorded text and transfer access should be checked.** This is the first known recording of “Nkosi Sikelel’ iAfrika”, performed by Solomon Tshekisho Plaatje with Sylvia Colenso on piano at Hayes, Middlesex, on 16 October 1923. The recording is part of a two-song side: “Pesheya Ko Tukela” (a Hlubi folk song) followed by “Nkosi Sikelel’ iAfrika”. The anthem is not labelled on the original disc according to the South African Audio Archive's discographical account, so preserve the composite-side identity.

## Recording identity

- **Singer:** Sol T. Plaatje.
- **Accompaniment:** Sylvia Colenso, piano.
- **Recording date/location:** 16 October 1923, Hayes, Middlesex, United Kingdom.
- **Label / catalogue:** Zonophone 4168.
- **Matrix:** Bb3640-2.
- **Side contents:** “a) Peshaya Ko Tukela” (Across the Tugela, Hlubi folk song); “b) Nkosi Sikelel’ iAfrika” (Native National Hymn in Zulu/isiZulu and isiXhosa).
- **Format:** 78 rpm shellac.
- **Archive identifier:** University of the Witwatersrand Historical Papers Research Archive, ZA HPRA A1742-1 (part of Sol Plaatje recordings).
- **Transfer/access:** Wits archive lists a digital audio item; DISA links are provided by the South African Audio Archive. Current download and reuse permissions have not been independently checked.
- **Do not infer:** the entire later national anthem is not present. The familiar additional isiXhosa verses by Samuel E. Mqhayi were added in 1927, after this recording.

## Text and political context

The original first stanza, written by Enoch Sontonga in isiXhosa in 1897, is a prayer asking God to bless Africa, lift its glory and hear the people's petitions. It is not a direct accusation or revenge lyric. Its relevance to this archive comes from its political and collective function: it was sung at the 8 January 1912 founding meeting of the South African Native National Congress (SANNC), and by the early 1920s it had become a major anthem of African political organisation. The SANNC adopted it as its official anthem in 1925 (some scholarship places the adoption earlier, in 1919). It became a rallying song at protest gatherings.

Plaatje himself was a founding member and first General Secretary of the SANNC. He had travelled to Britain with the 1914 delegation protesting the Natives Land Act of 1913, which restricted Black South Africans' land ownership. His 1923 recording session occurred during a visit to Britain on behalf of the SANNC. The recording therefore captures a key moment in the transnational circulation of a song that was both a Christian hymn and an emerging symbol of African political solidarity and resistance.

**The political context should not be mistaken for proof that the 1923 performance contains later verses or an explicit protest speech.** The recording preserves the early hymn text and its contemporary movement context. The 1927 verses must not be retroactively assigned to this take.

## Editorial interpretation

This is a strong example of protest through collective prayer, national solidarity and political ritual rather than accusation or retaliation. It widens the archive beyond North Atlantic protest traditions and foregrounds an African political movement and its own recorded voice. Keep the dual religious and political meanings intact.
