# GM-AM-008 — Tashjian Brothers, “Kerezmanis Togh” (Columbia E657/4955, 1910)

## Status

**Grade C — high-priority research lead; exact recorded text not yet established.** The performer, title, catalogue/issue association and session year are documented by the Armenian Museum of America. The Museum identifies the song as commonly known as “Hrayr Tzhokhki Yerke” (“Hrayr Tzhokhk’s Song”). A related Hrayr memorial text is preserved in Houshamadyan, but it is not a transcript of this Columbia side.

## Recording identity

- **Performer:** Tashjian Brothers — Mardiros, Nishan and Levon Der Sarkis.
- **Title on discography:** “Kerezmanis Togh”.
- **Common/related title:** “Hrayr Tzhokhki Yerke” / “Hrayr Tzhokhk’s Song”, according to the Armenian Museum.
- **Label and issue:** Columbia E657/4955.
- **Session:** New York, autumn 1910.
- **Release:** 1911.
- **Language:** Armenian.
- **Matrix/runout:** Not independently established beyond the Museum's catalogue/issue notation. Request label and runout photographs.
- **Recording carrier / transfer:** Not inspected. No audio auditioned or downloaded by ChatGPT.

The Armenian Museum says the Tashjian Brothers recorded 28 songs in autumn 1910 and identifies “Kerezmanis Togh” as a song about the fedayi Hrayr Tzhokhk. Its discography pairs this side with “Yerp vor Patsvin [Giligia]” at E657-4963. Preserve the side distinction: E657-4955 is the target here.

## Textual and thematic evidence

Houshamadyan's Sassoun / Alahaydoyan collection preserves a song titled “Sasnou Kavar (Hrayr Tjokhki Hishadagin)” — “Land of Sassoun (In Memory of Hrayr Tzhokhk)”. The text praises Sassoun, names revolutionary figures, describes conflict with Ottoman forces, and includes a speaker threatening to destroy the Ottoman army. Houshamadyan attributes this particular text to an ARF-affiliated family from Shenig in the Moush Valley and cites the ARF Archives in Watertown, Massachusetts. This is valuable regional and community provenance, but it does **not** establish that these exact verses occur on Columbia E657/4955.

A separate Houshamadyan Sassoun text, “Tartsyal Paylets”, has already been catalogued as GM-AM-007. Do not merge its explicit revenge refrain with this Hrayr memorial song. The shared names, regional repertoire and overlapping revolutionary figures are not proof that the lyrics or melodies are identical.

## Editorial interpretation

The song family belongs to the Armenian fedayi / Sassoun revolutionary-memory repertoire. Its likely relevance is collective resistance, anti-imperial grievance and armed defence, rather than a simple personal revenge narrative. Hrayr Tzhokhk's memorialisation must be situated within Ottoman Armenian regional history and the diasporic preservation of that repertoire. Do not flatten the song into a generic “revenge song” or treat later political uses as the only meaning.

**No mix approval.** Keep at Grade C until an Armenian-language transcription of the exact Columbia master is compared with the regional text and reviewed by a knowledgeable reader. Any liner note should identify the 1930 Houshamadyan source witness and the fact that the Columbia master has not yet been transcribed.

## 2026-11-19 — Armenian-language source-witness clarification

The Armenian-language version of Houshamadyan's Alahaydoyan collection gives the title in original script as **«Սասնու գաւառ (Հրայր Դժոխքի յիշատակին)»** (“Land of Sassoun [in memory of Hrayr Tzhokhk]”). It clarifies that the witness is a cassette recorded on **12 October 1979 in Talin, Armenia**, given by Babken Sassouni to collector Petros Alahaydoyan in 1986. A small group of Sassoun-origin singers performs; the page says only Samuel Torosian is named at the start of the tape. The transcription includes a line in which the speaker vows to destroy the Ottoman army, supporting an armed-resistance mechanism for this **separate 1979 text**.

This is not a transcript of Columbia E657/4955 and must not be merged with “Kerezmanis Togh” / “Hrayr Tzhokhki Yerke”. Houshamadyan's editorial note also says some performance wording was normalised against published songbooks rather than transcribed verbatim. That makes the 1979 page a valuable regional/textual witness, but not a verbatim lyric transcript of the cassette in every passage and not proof of the 1910 Columbia side's wording. A 2020 YouTube upload by Gohar Mkrtchyan presents a later public version of this 1979 cassette performance; it is not the Columbia master.
