# GM-OT-003 — Jacob Algava, “Hermanos mis queridos” (Gramophone, March 1909)

## Identification

- Performer: Jacob Algava (Sephardic singer, Salonica / Thessaloniki).
- Judeo-Spanish title: “Hermanos mis queridos” (modernized spelling sometimes “Hermanos mis keridos”; “My dear brothers”).
- Original 78 title in the EMI Archive Trust / Hebrew University discography: “Ermanos mis keridos”.
- Label and issue: Gramophone. The Hebrew University booklet gives Gramophone 6-12650; Kounadis gives Gramophone 12750 b – 2-12650. Preserve this discrepancy pending label/runout inspection.
- Matrix: 12750 b, according to Kounadis.
- Recording date/location: March 1909, Thessaloniki, according to Kounadis.
- Language: Judeo-Spanish (Ladino).
- Duration: 3:25 in the Hebrew University 2020 anthology.
- Access: included in *Eastern Mediterranean Judeo-Spanish Songs from the EMI Archive Trust (1907–1912)* (2020), CD 1, track 3. Streaming listings exist; no freely downloadable original transfer or exact archive file route established.

## Text and thematic evidence

The Hebrew University publication identifies the song as a Judeo-Spanish adaptation / abridgement of Joseph Pinto's “Cante nuevo por la constitución,” published in the Istanbul Judeo-Spanish periodical *El Djugueton* on 10 November 1908 and sung to the melody of the Turkish song “Yaşasın Hürriyet adalet” (“Long live freedom, justice”). It links the song to the reinstatement of the 1876 Ottoman Constitution in 1908 after the Young Turk revolt.

The publication transcribes the recorded text. It begins:
- “Trenta años pasaron” — “Thirty years passed”
- “muchas almas mataron” — “many souls they killed”
- “A la mar los echaron” — “they threw them into the sea”
- “muchas madres lloraron” — “many mothers wept”

The refrain celebrates “Viva la libertá” and “Viva la hermandad / con la justedad” (approximately, “Long live freedom” and “long live brotherhood with justice”). Later stanzas name Niyazi and Enver Bey and describe demanding and taking freedom. Keep the 1908 printed long text distinct from the 1909 recorded abridgement; the 2020 scholarly publication is the source for the recorded text transcription.

This is a substantial collective-grievance text: it remembers deaths, expulsions/deportations and bereaved mothers, then celebrates constitutional liberty and solidarity. Its political stance is specific: the song celebrates the Young Turk constitutional movement and its leaders. Do not treat it as an uncomplicated endorsement of later Committee of Union and Progress rule, or as evidence that all Ottoman communities experienced the constitutional order equally. Read it as a moment-specific Sephardic Ottoman political song, in Ladino.
