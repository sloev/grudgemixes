## Historical and thematic assessment

“Railroad Bill” is a Black-origin blues ballad associated in song and story with a fugitive train robber known as Railroad Bill, often identified as Morris Slater, though the identity is disputed. The American National Biography notes that the ballad circulated among field hands, turpentine-camp workers, prisoners and other Black working-class communities in the Deep South before appearing in print in 1911; the first commercial recording was made by Gid Tanner and Riley Puckett in 1924. https://academic.oup.com/american-national-biography/reference/63042/reference-article-abstract/570545184

Ballad of America cautions that only a small subset of collected verses refer specifically to Slater's activities and that the song's origins may predate the historical outlaw: https://balladofamerica.org/railroad-bill/

The title and outlaw narrative may suggest resistance to railroads or law enforcement, but that is not enough to classify the Puckett performance as retaliation or revenge. The actual verses may focus on pursuit, violence, work refusal, or comic outlaw boasting. Until the exact recording is transcribed, the safest status is a thematic-borderline lead. The tradition's Black origins and its later white commercial recording history should be explicitly acknowledged; do not erase the source community or treat the white commercial version as the origin.
