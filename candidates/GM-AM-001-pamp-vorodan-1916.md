# “Pamp Vorodan” / “Bam Porotan” — Torcom Bézazian, Victor 69102 (1916)

**Status: PARTIALLY VERIFIED / NOT CLEARED.** This is a strong Armenian-language revenge-song candidate with an exact Library of Congress National Jukebox item, recording date, matrix, take, label and catalogue number. The underlying poem was published in 1850 and is by Ghevont Alishan (1820–1901); its musical setting is attributed by Armenian Institute researcher Haig Utidjian to Pietro Bianchini (1828–1905). The LOC item credits H. Sinanian as arranger. The 1916 recording's exact sung text has not yet been compared with the 1850 poem, and the LOC access audio is not yet proven to be an unedited raw shellac transfer. No commercial rights are cleared.

## Exact recording object

- **Performer:** Torcom Bézazian, male vocal solo / tenor.
- **Title in LOC catalogue:** “Pamp vorodan”; other title “Armenian march.”
- **Label/catalogue:** Victor 69102.
- **Matrix/take:** B-18462, take 3.
- **Recording date/location:** 1916-10-11, New York City.
- **Accompaniment:** King's Orchestra; Edward T. King, conductor.
- **Arranger credit:** H. Sinanian.
- **Archive item:** Library of Congress National Jukebox, https://www.loc.gov/item/jukebox-19711/
- LOC describes an online audio and label image, and identifies the original source as the Johnson Victrola Museum, Delaware. Its rights advisory says the item is presented courtesy of Sony Music Entertainment or EMI Music and places responsibility for reuse/legal permissions on the user.
- The LOC web MP3/access audio is not, on current evidence, established as an unedited transfer of the original shellac. Obtain transfer/master documentation before treating it as an eligible source file.

## Composition, poem and author gate

Armenian Institute researcher Haig Utidjian identifies the correct Armenian title as **Բա՜մ. փորոտան** (*Bam Porotan*, approximately “Boom! They roar”), often miscatalogued/transliterated as “Pamp Vorodan” or “Bamb Vorotan.” He identifies the setting as Pietro Bianchini's composition, based on an excerpt from Ghevont Alishan's poem:

**Երգ ու գնացք զօրացն հայոց ընդ Վահանայ Մամիկոնենոյ ի Շաւարշական դաշտին վրէժք** — “Song and March of the Armenian Soldiers Under Vahan Mamikonian in the Field of Shavarshakan for Vengeance,” published at San Lazzaro, Venice, in **1850**.

- **Poet/lyricist:** Ghevont (Łewond) Alishan, 1820–1901.
- **Composer of the celebrated setting:** Pietro Bianchini, 1828–1905.
- Both author and composer died before the project's 1956 composition cutoff.
- The poem's historical-poetic frame is a rallying of Armenian soldiers under Vahan Mamikonian to avenge Vardan Mamikonian's defeat in 451. This is a literary/commemorative song about a historical past, not a contemporary eyewitness report of the fifth-century events.
- The phrase “revenge” is explicit in the poem's full title, and the lyrics include **Հայրենավրէժքդ Հայկազունք** (a phrase commonly transliterated *Hayrenavrezhk't Haykazunk*) and repeated **ի վրէժ** (“for revenge / in revenge”) calls to arms. An Armenian-language specialist should confirm the transcription, morphology and idiomatic translation before liner-note publication.

## Variant and recording history — keep credits distinct

1. **Mardiros Tashjian, Columbia 1909:** the Armenian Museum of America lists “Pomp Orodan [Vorodan]” on Columbia E530 / 4099 among Mardiros's solo recordings. Its relationship to the exact 1916 Victor performance should be checked.
2. **Tashjian Brothers, Columbia 1910:** the museum lists “Pamp Vorodan” on E667, paired with “Togh Pche Kamin”; it reports the 1910 session was released in 1911.
3. **Torcom Bézazian, Columbia 1915:** Recording Pioneers' E-Series spreadsheet lists matrix 39818-1, “Pamp Vorodan,” author H. Simanian, Bézazian, baritone, orchestra, Armenian, recorded 5 February 1915 in New York. It also lists a further 1917 matrix 44706- (date/location shown as 1917 / NYC). The spreadsheet is a specialist secondary discography; verify label and issue details.
4. **Torcom Bézazian, Victor 1916:** LOC item above, matrix B-18462 take 3, Victor 69102. LOC credits H. Sinanian as arranger. The 1915 Columbia credit “H. Simanian” and 1916 LOC “H. Sinanian” likely reflect a transliteration/credit variant, but confirm against primary labels and specialist biographies before normalizing.
5. **Armenag Shah-Mouradian, Orfeon 1914 and Columbia 1917:** Armenian Institute research documents two other performances; the 1914 Constantinople performance was accompanied by Komitas at piano, and the 1917 New York recording by orchestra. These are separate recordings and arrangements. The later recording is catalogued as “National song,” without naming Bianchini.

The Armenian Institute article explains that Bianchini's setting became widely adopted in Armenian diaspora communities, and that numerous later arrangements exist. Therefore, do not assume every performance uses the same melody/arrangement or includes the same excerpt/stanzas.

## Taxonomy

**Calculated / collective revenge rhetoric; historical-heroic commemoration.** The poem explicitly frames armed mobilization as vengeance for an earlier defeat. It should not be labelled “spite”: the text does not establish self-costly harm as its mechanism. It is not a documentary account of the 451 battle. The exact 1916 performance's text and arrangement still need listening and comparison.

## Provisional liner-note direction (not final copy)

In this Armenian-language march, the word “revenge” is not an interpretive gloss imposed on a vague patriotic title. It is built into the poem's full title: a song and march for the Armenian soldiers under Vahan Mamikonian, in the field of Shavarshakan, “for vengeance.” Ghevont Alishan's poem was published in 1850; Pietro Bianchini's musical setting helped carry it into Armenian communal and diasporic repertoire. The text recalls a much earlier defeat and summons a later generation of fighters to answer it. The 1916 Victor recording by Torcom Bézazian is one witness among several: related versions were recorded in Constantinople and New York, with different performers and arrangements. The recording should be heard as a historical-heroic representation of collective retaliation, not as an eyewitness document of fifth-century warfare. The exact words and arrangement on this Victor take still require comparison with the poem and other surviving versions.

## LOC transfer-chain clarification (collection-level, not item-specific)

The LOC National Jukebox Data Package documentation now clarifies the general transfer workflow for the Jukebox's Recorded Sound Section material:
- Original format is mostly 78rpm shellac discs.
- Engineers select an optimal stylus for each disc; the initial mono-disc transfer is captured in two channels (both groove walls), then summed to mono in the digital audio workstation.
- Preservation masters are created as 24-bit / 96 kHz Broadcast Wave files.
- Access copies are made from the mono master; the data-package README says the downloadable files are 44.1 kHz MP3s. LOC's public “Making the National Jukebox” page describes an access BWF at 16-bit / 44.1 kHz as an intermediate, with 320/128 kbps MP3s generated for streaming.
- LOC notes that discs are cleaned and otherwise prepared before digitization; in cases with multiple copies, the best-condition disc is selected.

This materially improves the provenance picture: the Jukebox MP3 is a derivative from a preservation workflow, not an undocumented modern reissue. It does **not** establish that the accessible MP3 is an unedited/raw transfer, nor does the collection-level description prove every step applied identically to this specific Victor side. The 24/96 preservation master may be the appropriate source to request for evaluation, subject to LOC access and rights procedures. Ask the Recorded Sound Research Center for the item-specific transfer notes and master availability.

## Title correction and Sinanian setting / arrangement evidence (new pass)

### Correct Armenian title

Haig Utidjian's Armenian Institute study (2021) corrects the title to **Բա՜մ. փորոտան** (*Bam Porotan*), approximately **“Boom! They roar.”** The verb փորոտալ means “to roar, to blow,” with a deep/mighty sound. Utidjian notes that the title was repeatedly mis-transcribed as Բամբ որոտան and that Shah-Mouradian's 1914 recording audibly appears to sing “Pamp vorodan.” Thus:
- Preserve **“Pamp vorodan”** as the LOC catalogue title for Victor 69102.
- Index the work under the corrected Armenian title **Բա՜մ. փորոտան / Bam Porotan**.
- Record “Pamp Vorodan,” “Pamp Porodan,” “Bamb Vorotan” and related spellings as historical/catalogue variants, not as the preferred normalized title.
- The corrected title itself is not a revenge claim; the revenge classification comes from Alishan's full poem title and narrative frame.

### H. Sinanian: setting, harmonisation and arrangement

The 1909 *Bazmavep* volume reproduces contemporary press notices (one attributed to *Stamboul*, apparently dated 21 February 1908, and another to *La Turquie*) reporting that Maestro H. Sinanian had put the Armenian song “Pamp Vorodan” to music, and that the score was for sale at music shops in Pera, Constantinople. This is evidence that a Sinanian musical setting/edition circulated by 1908–09. The 1914 *Handes Amsorya* bibliography lists H. Sinanian's *Հայկական երգեր / Chants populaires arméniens*, including No. 5 “Pamp Vorodan” and No. 6 “Dalvorig,” described as harmonised and arranged for piano and voice or violin ad libitum. The LOC Victor 1916 item credits H. Sinanian specifically as **arranger**.

This evidence needs to be reconciled with Utidjian's identification of Pietro Bianchini (1828–1905) as composer of the celebrated setting of Alishan's poem. A cautious interpretation is that Sinanian prepared a distinct harmonisation/arrangement or a local setting/edition of the song; the available sources do not yet establish whether his 1908 score is an independent composition, a re-harmonisation of Bianchini's melody, or a variant musical setting. Do not collapse “composer,” “harmoniser,” “arranger” and “publisher/editor” into one credit. For this Victor recording, retain the LOC credit “H. Sinanian — arranger”; separately note Bianchini's celebrated setting and the open question of the precise musical version used on the 1916 take.

The 1909 press notices are reproduced in *Bazmavep* (1909), pp. 135–136, in a section quoting Constantinople press notices. Their date/issue context should be checked against the original *Stamboul* and *La Turquie* issues if publication chronology becomes material. The notice's “for sale in music shops at Pera” supports circulation of a printed musical setting by the period, but does not prove the Victor 69102 issue date.

## Separate earlier recording branch discovered — Mademoiselle Rosalie, Favorite (1911)

A distinct Favorite Record issue of “Pamp Vorodan” by Mademoiselle Rosalie (Rozali Benliyan), recorded in Constantinople and catalogued by the MGK Collection as a 1911 release, has now been identified. Its photographed label appears to show catalogue 1-56211 and a runout inscription provisionally read as 4456-1; the label itself identifies Armenian, soprano with orchestra, Rosalie, Constantinople and “Pamp vorodan.” The collection record provides an Armenian transcription containing explicit revenge language, also transcribed for a separate 1917 Armenag Shah-Mouradian Columbia item. This strengthens the song-text history but does not verify the exact text on Bézazian's 1916 Victor take. Keep the Rosalie disc as a separate recording candidate and do not conflate performers, labels, matrices or arrangements. ## 2026-11-17 — Columbia version-family cross-check

The Armenian Museum of America’s full discography adds two earlier, distinct Tashjian performances of the same Alishan/Bianchini song family:

1. **Mardiros Der Sarkis Tashjian, Columbia E530-4099 (1909)** — listed in the museum’s 1909 solo discography as “Pomp Orodan [Vorodan]”, paired with “Thunderer March”. The museum dates the session family to 1909 but does not supply a matrix or exact take for this side.
2. **Tashjian Brothers, Columbia E667 (recorded autumn 1910; issued 1911)** — “Pamp Vorodan”, one of the 28 songs recorded by Mardiros with Nishan and Levon. The museum gives the catalogue number but no matrix or side-specific label/runout image in the text.

These are distinct performances and Columbia issue objects, not the 1916 Victor 69102 master. The museum’s account identifies the brothers’ 1910 performances as three-part vocal harmonies and records that the 1910 discs were issued in 1911. Preserve all three objects as a version family; **do not add the 1909 and 1910 performances as separate new-lead counts** because they are versions of the same composition/ballad already represented by this baseline candidate.

The 1916 Victor object remains the best-defined individual recording: Victor 69102, matrix B-18462, take 3, recorded 11 October 1916 in New York with King's Orchestra under Edward T. King. The poem’s 1850 publication and explicit vengeance frame remain supported by the Armenian Institute research already cited above. Exact correspondence between the 1916 performance and the full poem is still not established.
