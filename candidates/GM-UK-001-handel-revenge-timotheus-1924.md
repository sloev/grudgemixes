# Candidate: “Revenge! Timotheus Cries” — Handel, *Alexander's Feast* (Vocalion X-9560, 1924/25)

**Evidence level: B — PROBABLE CANDIDATE. Date: PROBABLE DATE. Audio and rights: unresolved / not cleared.**

## Why it matters

This is a particularly strong example of music represented as an instrument of retaliation. In Handel's *Alexander's Feast*, the singer Timotheus evokes the Furies and the ghosts of Greeks slain in earlier wars with Persia. His song moves Alexander toward burning Persepolis, framed by the poem as vengeance for Greek dead. The aria is not simply an aggressive title: the libretto explicitly stages a remembered injury, summoned dead, and a retaliatory act. It is a fictionalized ancient-historical narrative mediated through John Dryden's 1697 ode and Newburgh Hamilton's adaptation, not a neutral record of Alexander's actual motives.

## Recording and discographical evidence

- **Performer:** Malcolm McEachern, bass; Aeolian Orchestra.
- **Label/catalogue:** Vocalion X-9560, 10-inch 78rpm, issued in the United Kingdom.
- **Session/matrix:** specialist Vocalion sessionography lists London, **October 1924?**, matrix/session numbers **03724** and **03725**, with “Revenge Timotheus cries” parts 1 and 3 and “Behold a ghastly band” part 2; issue X-9560 is linked to 03724 and 03725X. Exact takes and the structure of the issued sides need physical-copy confirmation.
- **Publication:** the UK Vocalion catalogue's **1926 edition** (covering issues through November 1925) lists X-9560 as “Revenge! Timotheus Cries,” *Alexander's Feast* (Handel), parts I and II, with Aeolian Orchestra, bass Malcolm McEachern; its foreword says the edition contains all records issued up to and including November 1925, and the entry says replay part I to complete the aria. This is retrospective catalogue evidence that the issue was in circulation by the November 1925 catalogue cut-off, not a catalogue physically published in 1925.
- **Cross-check:** a later McEachern discography independently lists X-9560 and matrices/issues 03724/03725. A 2010 specialist review says McEachern recorded the aria on Vocalion in the early 1920s and comments on the 1924 studio circumstances; useful corroboration, not primary session evidence.
- No exact item-level public archive audio or preservation-transfer record has yet been located. Do not claim that the audio has been heard or that the two parts are complete without inspecting the physical issue.

## Composition and narrative chronology

The British Library's Royal Music Collection catalogue describes Handel's autograph *Alexander's Feast* (HWV 75), libretto by Newburgh Hamilton after John Dryden, dated 1736. The work was first performed in London on 19 February 1736. Dryden's ode dates to 1697; Hamilton adapted it and supplied additional material. The aria's text is securely pre-1926.

The historical referent is the ancient banquet celebrating Alexander's victory over Persia. The oratorio's dramaturgy presents Timotheus's music as capable of changing Alexander's emotional state and inciting revenge for Greeks killed in earlier Persian wars. Yale's Institute of Sacred Music notes the aria's role in conjuring slain Greek warriors and the subsequent burning of Persepolis. Keep the distinction between ancient history, Dryden's poetic invention, Hamilton's adaptation and Handel's musical setting explicit.

## Thematic classification

**Collective / inherited grievance; retaliatory incitement; revenge through performance.** The aria summons the Furies and the unburied Greek dead, then the following numbers move toward vengeance. The broader sequence, rather than the isolated title alone, explains the mechanism. If the Vocalion issue contains only the aria and not the following “Give the Vengeance Due” recitative or Thais episode, describe the latter as dramatic context, not recorded content.

## Draft liner note direction

A singer's revenge can become a crowd's revenge. In *Alexander's Feast*, Timotheus performs before Alexander after the conquest of Persia. The music first changes the king's mood; then it calls up the Furies and the ghosts of Greeks killed in earlier wars. The aria “Revenge! Timotheus Cries” turns those dead into an argument for retaliation, and the wider ode proceeds to Alexander's burning of Persepolis. Handel's 1736 setting uses John Dryden's 1697 poem, adapted by Newburgh Hamilton. Nearly two centuries later, Australian bass Malcolm McEachern recorded the aria for Vocalion in London, probably in October 1924. The 1925 Vocalion catalogue lists the two-part issue X-9560 and warns listeners to replay the first part to complete the aria. This is not a recording of an ancient event: it is a modern performance of a literary account of how music can manufacture the desire for payback.

## 1926 catalogue wording clarification

The Vocalion source is the **1926 edition** of the catalogue, not a catalogue published in 1925. Its foreword explicitly says it lists records issued up to and including November 1925. Its X-9560 entry lists parts I and II, Aeolian Orchestra and bass Malcolm McEachern, with part I replayed to complete the aria. The 1924-10? session date and 03724/03725 matrix/session references remain specialist-discography evidence and provisional. See the item entry in the catalogue (pp. 90 and 137): https://78rpm.club/wp-content/uploads/2015/10/Vocalion-Records-Catalogue-1925-UK.pdf and the sessionography https://www.78opera.com/files/ARTIST_M.pdf .
