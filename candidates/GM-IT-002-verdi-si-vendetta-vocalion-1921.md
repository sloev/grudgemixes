# Candidate lead: “Sì, vendetta” — Verdi, *Rigoletto* (Vocalion, ca. 1921)

**Status: PARTIALLY VERIFIED — exact archive item and issue identification located; exact recording date/matrix and direct audio/transfer details unresolved. NOT CLEARED.**

## Why it matters

The Act III duet “Sì, vendetta” (“Yes, vengeance”) from Verdi's *Rigoletto* is an explicit operatic statement of revenge. Florida Atlantic University's Recorded Sound Archives (FAU RSA) has a digitized 78rpm item titled “Don Giovanni - La ci darem; Rigoletto - Si vendetta (Yes, My Vengeance),” with the second track attributed to Verdi. The archive item identifies catalogue number **60019**, performers **Evelyn Scotney** and **Giacomo Rimini**, 78 rpm, 10-inch.

A specialist Vocalion discography identifies Scotney's 1921 US Vocalion session entry **7176** as “Rigoletto: Si vendetta (w. Rimini),” issued as catalogue numbers **30146 / 60019**. This supports the connection between the archive item and the 1921 performance, but the session date is only tentatively given as March 1921 and no exact matrix/take is supplied in the evidence located so far.

## Taxonomy

**Calculated revenge / retaliatory justice.** In the opera, Gilda and Rigoletto respond to the Duke's seduction and betrayal; Rigoletto's plan for vengeance culminates in the fatal plot involving Sparafucile. The duet is not simply an abstract declaration: it is embedded in a specific dramatic situation of sexual betrayal, coercion, class power and planned retribution. The opera is a fictional representation, not a historical account.

## Liner-note direction (draft only; revise after listening)

“Si, vendetta” gives revenge a duet form. In Verdi's *Rigoletto*, the declaration belongs to a plot shaped by the Duke's abuse of power and Gilda's betrayal, but the response is not a spontaneous outburst: Rigoletto has already set a fatal plan in motion. The Vocalion recording pairs soprano Evelyn Scotney with baritone Giacomo Rimini, accompanied by the Aeolian Orchestra. A specialist discography places the session in the United States around March 1921 and links it to Vocalion catalogue 60019; FAU's Recorded Sound Archives preserves a digitized item under that catalogue number. The archive's own metadata does not supply a recording date or matrix, so the sessionography remains a provisional identification rather than a complete discographic record. The aria's force lies in the collision between a public promise of vengeance and the private catastrophe that follows. It is a theatrical construction of revenge, not evidence of an actual case.
