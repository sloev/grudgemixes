Alfred Graham's 1894 patent described a way to make a continuous musical tone from electrical feedback. A telephone transmitter and receiver were coupled by a flexible tube; vibrations from the receiver altered the resistance of a carbon-granule microphone, which fed the signal back into the receiver. Close the circuit and the system sang to itself.

Graham suggested placing a perforated tube between the microphone and horn, effectively turning the feedback apparatus into an “electric flute” whose pitch could be changed by opening and closing holes. On 31 December 1895, Graham's apparatus was demonstrated during a Royal Institution lecture on sound, hearing and speech. The lecturer described it as containing the beginnings of a possible musical instrument independent of conventional mechanical sound production.

This was not a synthesizer in the modern sense, and no recording of the demonstration is known. It is an exceptionally early documented attempt to make electricity itself sustain a musical tone — a laboratory curiosity that never became an instrument.
