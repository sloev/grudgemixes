Johann Baptist Schalkenbach (1824–1910) came from Trier in Prussia and made his living first as a cloth merchant, then as a performer-inventor. His early 1862 *Piano-Orchestre* was a mechanical pile-up of harmonium, harmoniflute, bells, drums, gongs and whistles. It was not yet an electric instrument. At the Palace of St Cloud in 1862 he added electrical controls; by the 1870s his *Orchestre Militaire Electro Moteur* could trigger distant drums, cymbals, church bells, pistol shots and cannon effects from a stage console.

The storm was part machine, part theatrical trick: buckshot for rain, struck steel for thunder, and bellows blowing across a hollow copper ball for wind. He later wired audience seats to deliver electric shocks. Music critics hated the racket; music-hall audiences came to be startled. A 1875 fire destroyed the machine, but Schalkenbach rebuilt it. His act helped spawn a small industry of electrical music-hall imitators.

## Why this is not a public recording post yet

No recording of Schalkenbach's own performances has been located. This is a strong historical subject, but it cannot enter the recording collection until an eligible recording or a suitable documented later performance is found. The Cambridge article also corrects an important myth: the original 1862 machine was non-electrical; the electrical stage effects came later.
