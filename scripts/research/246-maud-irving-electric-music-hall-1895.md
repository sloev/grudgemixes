When Sandor Rosner, billed as Edwin Rousby, and his wife Maud Irving reached Britain in July 1895, their act resembled the electrical music-hall performances of Mephisto. But the focus had shifted: Irving was now the central figure at the electromusical console, operating the apparatus while the act presented electricity as spectacle.

Rousby later advertised a 1900 attraction called *Electricity* in Blackpool, inviting visitors to become performers themselves by pressing buttons, turning handles and pulling levers. The audience was no longer merely asked to marvel at invisible music; it could operate the machine.

The act sits at the end of a short-lived tradition in which electrical music was sold as theatrical mystery. Its women performers are particularly easy to lose from the history: Maud Irving and Susette D’Alcorn were not simply assistants standing beside a machine, but the people visibly playing it.
