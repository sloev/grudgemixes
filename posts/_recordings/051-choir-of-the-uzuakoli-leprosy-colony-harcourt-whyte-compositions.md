In 1919 Ikoli Harcourt Whyte, a fourteen-year-old in eastern Nigeria, was diagnosed with leprosy. In 1932 he walked a hundred miles — with two hundred others — to the Methodist leprosy settlement at Uzuakoli, and he stayed for the rest of his life, even after doctors declared him cured in 1949. There he worked with the medical director, Frank Davey, who taped songs from outlying villages and played them back to him; from those tunes Whyte built a new choral music for the Igbo language — more than two hundred hymns of solace and hope, no instruments to crowd the words. He formed a choir from the patients themselves; it toured Nigeria and sang for British dignitaries, and during the civil war his hymns circulated as a source of courage. He died in a road accident in May 1977; after his death, the colony choir committed his work to vinyl. Those records have never resurfaced online — below, his best-known hymn, "Bia Nu Ndi Enyim N'ile", sung today. A Nigerian university chair of choral music carries his name.
