In 1984 a workman renovating an apartment in Copacabana, Rio de Janeiro, opened an electrical box and found dozens of identical cassette tapes hidden behind it — fifty of them, unbranded, with no markings at all. The worker, Moacyr, took the box home instead of to the police. Years later his daughter, the researcher Regina H. Castro McGowan, catalogued what she came to call a FITA: a seventeen-track programme of Latin American protest music, nine of the tracks in Spanish — Víctor Jara, Violeta Parra, Quilapayún, Tiemponuevo, Mercedes Sosa — plus Brazilian songs by Carlos Lyra, Chico Buarque and Geraldo Vandré, closing with the live "El pueblo unido jamás será vencido". The newest songs dated from 1975, and every one of them was banned in Brazil. The apartment, the article argues, was probably an aparelho — a clandestine safehouse — and whoever made the fifty copies never came back for them. The tapes had waited, hidden, through two decades of dictatorship, for a renovation to find them.
