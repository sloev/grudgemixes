On 6 July 1889, at the Neurological Society of Paris, Dr Henri Charles Victor Maurice Dupont stood up to propose something new: recording the delusions of the insane on Edison's phonograph. Dupont, a physician little remembered today — intern at the Vincennes asylum, then head of the electricity laboratory at Sainte-Anne — had made his experiments in Alix Joffroy's laboratory at that Paris hospital. Recordings of grandiose and persecutory delusions had already piled up there until, he said, the hospital was "a museum of delusions". Before his audience he presented samples: grandiose delusions spoken by a general paralytic, speech disturbances in multiple sclerosis, Parkinson's and Friedreich's disease, verbal hallucinations first described by Jules Séglas. The phonograph, he argued, could "preserve and reproduce in a lively way" what writing flattened — the intonation, the accent, "the animated mimicry of the illness". It was the first such proposal in France, and none of those recordings is known to survive today: the museum of delusions is silent.
