In September 1958 a Copenhagen policeman named Anders Enevig began carrying a tape recorder on his rounds. As a constable on Nørrebro he had come to know the people the city looked through — moonshiners, vagrants, the men and women living rough — and he started taping their songs and stories. He kept going for twenty-five years, working with Dansk Folkemindesamling, the national folklore archive: 205 tapes, close to 400 hours, made between 1958 and 1983 among Travellers, showmen, circus artists and vagabonds across Denmark. Among the voices is Agnes Sambleben, whose ballad he captured in 1959. That same year Enevig wrote a critical newspaper piece about Blushøj, the Valby camp where Travelling families lived in wretched conditions; the following decades brought books on Travellers and circus folk and a campaign that saved the Cirkusbygningen. The archive held what Danish folklore collecting had long skipped: the music of people treated as a social problem, preserved in full only because one policeman chose to listen.
