# `Аэлита` / Aelita: Queen of Mars (1924) — music and accompaniment

## Scope and identification

- Film: `Аэлита` / `Aelita` / *Aelita: Queen of Mars* (1924)
- Director: Yakov Protazanov (Яков Протазанов)
- Composer associated with the original score: Valentin Yakovlevich Kruchinin (Валентин Яковлевич Кручинин)
- Research scope: evidence for the original silent-film music, surviving related material, contemporary performance practice, and later soundtrack substitution. This note does not claim to have watched or listened to any film or recording.

## Original score and surviving evidence

**VERIFIED (scholarly source reporting archival/documentary evidence):** Hannah C. J. McLaughlin identifies Kruchinin as the composer of the 1924 film's original musical accompaniment. Her 2024 peer-reviewed article states that the film score is lost and that the film's cue sheets do not appear to survive in written or recorded form. The article distinguishes that missing score from surviving related material: Kruchinin's short piano piece also titled `Aelita`, Op. 24.

**WELL-SUPPORTED:** The surviving piano piece is a separate, short work, not an established surviving extract or reconstruction of the full film score. Its title page reportedly describes it as a composition “for the worldwide picture AELITA”; its publisher's designation was “choreographic sketch” (`khoreograficheskiy eskiz`). McLaughlin cautions that there is no sure evidence that this piece itself accompanied the film. Its likely publication around 1924–25 is an inference from the title-page wording, not a securely dated publication fact.

**UNKNOWN:** Whether any complete or partial score, orchestral parts, cue sheets, recording, or other soundtrack material survives outside the collections and documentary trail discussed in the article. The article's finding that the score and cue sheets do not appear to have survived should not be inflated into proof that no related material exists anywhere.

## Contemporary screenings and performance

**WELL-SUPPORTED, based on the article's citations to contemporary press and archival material:** McLaughlin reports that Kruchinin entrusted his score to conductor D. S. Blok in a note dated 28 October 1924, held in the Russian State Archive of Literature and Art (RGALI), fond 675, opis 2, delo 340, folio 7. The article also cites advertisements in *Izvestiya* (12 and 13 November 1924) for full orchestral accompaniment at the film's Moscow premiere at the Ars Theatre under Blok. A screening at Cinema Forum reportedly had orchestral work under A. G. Berezovsky.

The article describes silent-film accompaniment as variable between venues: even when a score existed, local resources and performers could alter what audiences heard. It therefore cautions against assuming that Kruchinin's full orchestral score was heard at every screening as the film circulated.

**UNKNOWN / UNVERIFIED:** Some secondary accounts have claimed Dmitri Shostakovich played piano for *Aelita* screenings in Leningrad. McLaughlin explicitly notes that she could not find primary-source confirmation. Do not repeat this as a fact without new documentary evidence.

## What the musical evidence does — and does not — tell us

McLaughlin analyses the surviving piano piece as a slow foxtrot / “eccentric dance,” with syncopation and ragtime-like left-hand figures. This is an analysis of the extant promotional/related piano piece, not a description of the lost film score as a whole.

A 1924 review discussed in the article characterizes the Martian scenes as having an “opera-sweet” quality and refers to an overture and theatrical musical atmosphere. This provides contemporary reception evidence for aspects of the screening, but does not establish the complete instrumentation, cue sequence, or exact music heard in every theatre.

Do not infer theremin, synthesizers, “space-age” sonorities, or a futuristic musical language from the film's science-fiction subject. McLaughlin specifically argues that such tropes were not standard in 1924 and that Kruchinin's surviving piece instead reflects popular dance idioms.

## Later soundtrack / version distinction

**WELL-SUPPORTED:** McLaughlin reports that the 2004 Ruscico re-release replaces Kruchinin's original music with a mixture of works by Alexander Scriabin, Igor Stravinsky, and Alexander Glazunov. This later accompaniment must not be presented as Kruchinin's original score or as evidence of what audiences heard in 1924.

**UNKNOWN:** Exact edition identifiers, release packaging, track-by-track cue mapping, and whether other restorations or releases use different replacement accompaniments. These require direct edition-level verification.


## Princeton University Library surviving-score lead (2026-11-16)

**WELL-SUPPORTED:** Princeton University Library's Digital Collections browse index independently exposes an item titled *Aëlita / Valentin Kruchinin* and indicates four associated digital files. The index entry is consistent with McLaughlin's identification of Princeton University Library as the source of the reproduced cover and score images. This gives the project a concrete institutional collection lead for the surviving Op. 24 piano piece, rather than relying only on the article's figure reproduction.

**Important limits:** the browse page is a discovery/index page, not a complete item-level catalogue record. In this check, the exact item identifier, printed publication date, publisher statement transcribed from the object, rights/reuse terms, and downloadable master files were not independently established. Do not infer that the score is public domain or that its images may be republished. The score remains distinct from the lost full film score and is not an audio recording candidate for the soundtrack collection.
