# Latin American early corrido screening — 2026-11-15

## Scope

A focused first pass on early Mexican corridos and commercially issued Edison cylinders, following the project-wide rule to search outside Europe and North America from the outset. The aim here was to locate pre-1926 narrative recordings with explicit grievance, betrayal, retaliation or revenge, not merely to collect early corridos.

## Strong chronological evidence: Mexico City Edison cylinder campaign, 1904

The Edison Phonograph Monthly's 1904 catalogue lists a group of Mexican recordings made in Mexico City under National Phonograph Company recording experts. Among the baritone solos by Rafael Herrera Robinson are:
- **18510–18511**, *El Descarrilamiento*, parts 1–2, corrido;
- **18512–18513**, *Ignacio Parra*, parts 1–2, corrido;
- **18514**, *A Elena*, corrido, by Maximiano Rosales and Leopoldo Picazo;
- **18515**, *Heraclio Bernal*, corrido, with guitar accompaniment, by Rafael Herrera Robinson;
- **18518**, *Jesús Leal*, corrido, by Rafael Herrera Robinson.

The 1904 catalogue is a primary commercial catalogue witness to the titles, format and artist credits; it lists Edison record numbers, not modern shellac issue numbers. A later Mexican corrido historiography study gives the same sequence and notes that the cylinders were commercially issued. Agustín Gurza's Frontera essay independently says the two outlaw ballads *Heraclio Bernal* and *Ignacio Parra* were recorded in Mexico City in 1904 by Herrera Robinson for Edison.

## Thematic screening: no candidate admitted

### *Heraclio Bernal* — Edison 18515 (1904): LEAD ONLY / NOT CANDIDATE

The subject is Heraclio Bernal (1855–1888), a Sinaloan outlaw and anti-Porfirian rebel whose career and death generated multiple corrido versions. A 1931 Mexican broadside catalogue at El Colegio de México identifies a *Corrido de Heraclio Bernal* as anonymous and gives an opening verse that dates the tragedy to 1882; a modern scholarly discussion describes the corrido cycle as anonymous and notes that versions differ. A later 1953 performance of a Bernal corrido narrates a government reward, Crispín García's betrayal of Bernal for money, and Bernal's capture/death. This later text is not evidence of the precise wording on Edison 18515.

The 1904 title is promising because the later tradition centres on betrayal by a companion and state violence, but no contemporary lyric text or item-level recording has been checked. The narrative may be a lament / outlaw commemoration / political resistance rather than revenge. No direct retaliation by Bernal is established in the evidence inspected. Keep as a lead only until the original cylinder is located and heard and its lyrics transcribed.

Sources:

### *Ignacio Parra* — Edison 18512–18513 (1904): LEAD ONLY / NOT CANDIDATE

The Edison catalogue establishes a two-part corrido by Rafael Herrera Robinson, but no exact original lyrics, historical referent or revenge mechanism were verified in this pass. Frontera describes Parra as a rebel active in the late 1880s during the Díaz dictatorship and notes that Herrera did not reprise the original Parra and Bernal ballads on later Victor/Columbia visits; James Nicolopulos suggested political pressure and a “seditious undercurrent” may explain the omission. This is important evidence of politically contentious repertoire, but not enough to classify the song as revenge, retaliation or a specific grievance narrative.

## Rights and eligibility notes

- The Edison catalogue numbers and 1904 date are strong evidence of early commercial release, but do not establish the survival of a particular cylinder or the exact fixation date of each master.
- A wax cylinder fits the project's source-format rule, but only an original or archival transfer with documented provenance is eligible; a modern commercial reissue is not.
- A modern Arhoolie CD compilation includes later performances and a historical booklet. It is a research aid, not a qualifying raw source. Smithsonian archive access restrictions and rights review also apply to its own collection.
- Commercial reuse rights for the 1904 Edison cylinders remain unassessed and must be checked by jurisdiction and by the particular surviving copy / transfer.

## Decision

No candidate admitted. The Mexican 1904 Edison catalogue is a significant global discovery route and produces two valuable outlaw-ballad leads, but exact original lyrics and audio are missing and the thematic mechanism is not yet sufficiently clear. Next steps: search the UCSB and Edison National Historical Park catalogues for 18512/18515; request original cylinder labels, matrix/recording ledger data and transfer notes; listen and transcribe before deciding whether either ballad belongs in the series.


## Focused follow-up: *Ignacio Parra* has a pre-1926 printed text and explicit “society avenged” variant

The initial screening note understated the strength of the *Ignacio Parra* lead. A follow-up search found a coherent cluster of printed and scholarly witnesses:

1. **1905 anonymous broadside.** Aurelio González cites *Corrido de Ignacio Parra*, a single-sheet broadside from Imprenta de Antonio Vanegas Arroyo, Mexico City, 1905. He identifies it as a text of circumstance praising Octaviano Meraz, “jefe bueno de Acordada,” and expressing the gratitude of the people of Durango; it casts Parra as a criminal. González quotes the final stanza of this 1905 text, in which the community is said to have gained from Parra's death and is invited to see how wrongdoers die. The article explicitly distinguishes this officialist version from corridos that side with the marginal outlaw.
2. **1914 anonymous broadside.** El Colegio de México preserves a two-page 1914 Vanegas Arroyo broadside, *Ya la autoridad echó garra al malvado Ignacio Parra*. Its headline refrain says Parra paid with his life, justice triumphed, and society is avenged. The full digital scan/transcription narrates Parra's crimes, the death of his lieutenant Refugio Alvarado, and Octaviano Meraz's pursuit and killing of Parra. This is an especially explicit contemporary statement of collective vengeance/punishment, but it is a later variant and not proof of the exact 1904 cylinder text.
3. **1925 anthology witness.** Higinio Vázquez Santa Ana's *Historia de la canción mexicana*, vol. 1 (Talleres Gráficos de la Nación, 1925), includes *Corrido de Ignacio Parra* at p.210. Aurelio González's article cites the 1924/25 Vázquez Santana collection, pp.210–213, as the source for the 1905 broadside text. This gives a pre-cutoff printed textual witness, though the original 1905 broadside and 1914 broadside are distinct versions.
4. **1904 commercial cylinder.** The Edison Phonograph Monthly catalogue lists *Ignacio Parra*, parts 1–2, Edison 18512–18513, as corridos by Rafael Herrera Robinson. This is the exact early recording lead. The 1904 catalogue and later corrido historiography establish the recording title, performer and record numbers, but the original cylinder's words have not yet been compared with either printed version.

### Classification and decision

This is now a **HIGH-PRIORITY LEAD / NEAR CANDIDATE**, not yet a candidate. The recorded title, performer, two-part structure and commercial Edison catalogue numbers are documented in 1904; an anonymous 1905 broadside supplies early traditional-repertoire provenance; a separate 1914 version makes the collective vengeance frame explicit. The likely mechanism is **collective punishment / state justice represented as society's vengeance**, rather than an individual's calculated revenge, personal spite or the outlaw's own retaliation. It is a politically situated officialist account, not a neutral record of the events or a transparent popular consensus. González's analysis cautions that the 1905 version praises Meraz and presents Parra as a criminal, while other corridos of the same outlaw tradition could take the opposite side.

Do not quote the 1914 “society avenged” line as if it were heard on the 1904 cylinder. Before candidate admission, obtain and hear Edison 18512–18513, transcribe both sides, and compare the recorded text with the 1905 broadside and 1925 anthology; separately compare the 1914 variant. Locate an original/archival cylinder transfer and item-level transfer notes; determine territorial rights. The 1905/1914 printed witnesses support the composition/traditional provenance gate if the recording is shown to preserve that text or a related traditional variant.

### Date refinement from the original Edison trade journal

The 1904–05 volume of *Edison Phonograph Monthly* provides more precise chronology than the catalogue title alone:
- The volume identifies itself as **Vol. II, 1904–05**, with publication beginning in March 1904 and running through February 1905.
- In the January 1905 issue, the article “Seventy-one Mexican Records” says the 71 records were made in Mexico City under National Phonograph Company recording experts and that large numbers had already been sold in Mexico. It lists *Ignacio Parra* 18512–18513 and *Heraclio Bernal* 18515 under Rafael Herrera Robinson.
- The same issue reproduces Thomas A. Edison's letter to President Porfirio Díaz dated **2 September 1904**. Edison says he is sending an assortment of Gold Moulded records “recently made by prominent artists in Mexico City” and that Díaz should receive the first copies for his birthday on **15 September 1904**. Díaz's reply is dated 19 September 1904 and acknowledges receipt of the phonograph and records.
- This establishes that the Mexican recording campaign had produced its first copies by 15 September 1904, and that the catalogue list was commercially available by January 1905. It does not identify the exact recording day of *Ignacio Parra* 18512–18513, so retain the item-level recording date as **Mexico City, by 15 September 1904 (session date not identified)**, with catalogue publication January 1905.

Source: *Edison Phonograph Monthly*, Vol. II, No. 11 (January 1905), pp.4–5, “Seventy-one Mexican Records” and “President Diaz's Birthday Gift”: https://www.worldradiohistory.com/Archive-All-Audio/Edison-Phonograph/Edison-Phonograph-Monthly-1904-Vol-2.pdf


### Distribution evidence: Edison kept the Mexican corridos in its Mexico catalogue after withdrawing them from the U.S. foreign catalogue

A second primary trade source adds useful market context. The November 1907 *Edison Phonograph Monthly* article “Mexican Records Cut Out” says that, because demand among U.S. dealers was limited, the company would omit selected Mexican records from the next U.S. Foreign Record Catalogue while **continuing to list them in catalogues printed for use in Mexico**. Its table retains:
- 18510–18511, *El Descarrilamiento*;
- 18512–18513, *Ignacio Parra*;
- 18514, *A Elena*;
- 18515, *Heraclio Bernal*;
- and numerous other Robinson titles.

This corroborates that the Ignacio Parra and Heraclio Bernal records were part of a Mexico-facing commercial catalogue at least in November 1907. It also warns against assuming that absence from later U.S./European export catalogues means the records were withdrawn everywhere. It does not establish the exact original release date or prove a surviving copy.

Source: *Edison Phonograph Monthly*, November 1907, “Mexican Records Cut Out”: https://npgallery.nps.gov/GetAsset/8edec098-f1cd-435d-94b3-e817e83ef167/original


### Dating discrepancy for the Posada / “Ya la autoridad” broadside

The same *Ya la autoridad echó garra al malvado Ignacio Parra* image and four-line refrain are dated differently by two institutional records:
- El Colegio de México catalogues its two-page Vanegas Arroyo broadside as **1914**.
- The Amon Carter Museum catalogues José Guadalupe Posada's zinc-etching print with the same title and inscription as **1912**.
Until the physical copies, imprint details and collection histories are compared, record this print witness as **1912–1914 (institutional dating discrepancy)**, not as a securely dated 1914 issue. This does not affect the separate 1905 anonymous *Corrido de Ignacio Parra* witness.


## Critical textual and political-context correction — the 1904 cylinder is not established as the 1905 officialist broadside

A further check of the Frontera Collection's historical essay and the 2025 corrido historiography chapter requires a qualification to the earlier follow-up above.

- Frontera explicitly identifies the 1904 *Ignacio Parra* cylinder as one of two ballads about rebels active in the late 1880s during the Porfirio Díaz dictatorship. It says Rafael Herrera Robinson did not reprise either *Ignacio Parra* or *Heraclio Bernal* in later Victor/Columbia sessions, and reports James Nicolopulos's interpretation that political pressure and a “seditious undercurrent” explain the omission. This is a substantially different political framing from the officialist 1905 broadside.
- The 2025 chapter “El corrido en México: historiografía e instituciones” places Ignacio Parra among the nineteenth-century bandit/social-bandit protagonists represented in corridos. It separately establishes the 1904 Edison sequence and the two catalogue numbers 18512–18513, performed by Herrera Robinson.
- Aurelio González's analysis of the 1905 Vanegas Arroyo broadside describes that **printed witness** as praising Octaviano Meraz and presenting Parra as a criminal. The later 1912/1914 “Ya la autoridad echó garra” print is another witness with its own institutional dating discrepancy. None of those print sources has yet been demonstrated to reproduce the 1904 cylinder's text.

**Corrected interpretation:** the 1904 recording should provisionally be described as a commercially issued corrido about the rebel/outlaw Ignacio Parra, in a politically charged anti-Díaz context. The officialist 1905 broadside and later “society avenged” refrain demonstrate competing or variant public narratives about Parra's death, not the verified lyrics of the 1904 cylinder. Do not classify the recording itself as collective vengeance, nor quote the officialist refrain as its lyric. Its relevance to the *grudgemixes* taxonomy remains **UNRESOLVED**: it may concern social grievance, state repression, rebellion or revenge, but the precise recorded narrative and mechanism require the cylinder text.

The 1904 item remains **HIGH-PRIORITY LEAD / NOT A CANDIDATE / NOT CLEARED**. This replaces the earlier “HIGH-PRIORITY LEAD / NEAR CANDIDATE” wording in the preceding subsection. The broadside witnesses remain useful as evidence of competing textual memory, but must be kept separate from the Edison master unless a lyric comparison proves a relationship.

## Additional Edison Mexican corrido lead — *Macario Romero* (18638, listed 1905)

The newly available UCSB / American Discography Project 2025 reprint of Sydney H. Carter's *Catalog of Edison Foreign Series Cylinder Records* provides a useful checklist beyond the first 1904 group. Its Mexican series lists:
- **18638, *Macario Romero* — corrido**, performed by **Rafael Herrera Robinson and Leopoldo Picazo** (Carter, p.121 of the 2025 PDF; the same number and duo appear in the May 1905 *Edison Phonograph Monthly* Mexican catalogue).
- **18750, *El Desprecio* — corrido de tierra caliente**, performed by **R. Robinson with guitar** (Carter, p.127; also in the 1905 catalogue listing).

The *Macario Romero* narrative has a printed textual tradition, but the witnesses must be kept separate:
- El Colegio de México catalogues Eduardo Guerrero's one-sheet *Corrido de Macario Romero* (Corridos-0128), with opening “Hay hombres que son valientes, pero ninguno fue igual à don Macario Romero, que tuvo triste final.” Its publication date is not identified.
- A second one-sheet witness, anonymous and held in the Concha Michel collection (Corridos-0338), opens “Dice Macario Romero al capitán Villa Plata: concédame una licencia para ir a ver a mi chata.” Its publication date is also not identified.
- Jesús Iván Mora Muro's 2025 chapter quotes a variant in which Macario is lured to a dance, disarmed and shot “a traición” (by treachery); the final line has Jesusita Llamas say “ahora sí quedamos bien, ya mataron a Macario, ahora mátenme a mí también.” This may suggest a score-settling or revenge dimension, but the speaker's motive and the meaning of “quedamos bien” require the full text and historical context. Do not reduce the narrative to revenge from this fragment alone.
- Álvaro Ochoa Serrano's 2025 biographical study confirms the Edison cylinder number and duo and describes the Rosales/Robinson repertoire; it does not establish the exact words sung on the cylinder. Another modern source attributes a *Macario Romero* corrido to Jesús Ábrego and Picazo around 1908, a different attribution/chronology that should not be merged with the 18638 Edison performance without a text and master comparison.

**Status: LEAD ONLY / NOT A CANDIDATE / NOT CLEARED.** The catalogue record and early commercial issue are now well supported, and there is a pre-1926 corrido tradition around Macario Romero. The thematic evidence is betrayal/ambush/murder with a possible but ambiguous score-settling line; it is not yet enough to classify the Edison performance as revenge, retaliation or spite. Composition authorship, exact text, fixation date, original-cylinder transfer provenance and territorial rights remain open.

*El Desprecio* is a separate **TITLE-ONLY LEAD**. Its corrido designation and performer are documented, but title alone does not establish a grievance narrative.

### Later Mexico-market catalogue cross-check (November 1907) — catalogue-number discrepancy

The November 1907 *Edison Phonograph Monthly* Mexico catalogue list contains **18634, *Macario Romero*** (Robinson y Picazo), while **18638 is *Gigantes y Cabezudos—Romanza*** (Goyzueta). This conflicts with the 1905 catalogue / Carter 2025 reprint references above, which identify **18638 *Macario Romero*** by Herrera Robinson and Picazo. The discrepancy may reflect catalogue renumbering, a transcription error in one witness, or a later catalogue error; the evidence presently available does not decide which. Do not silently harmonise the numbers or assume the 1907 entry is the same cylinder/master. Record the Edison number as **18638 (1905 catalogue/Carter witness; requires primary image re-check)** and **18634 (November 1907 Mexico catalogue)** until the original catalogue scans and any cylinder labels/matrices are compared. The November 1907 list also includes **18750 *El Desprecio*** (Robinson). It confirms the titles' presence in a Mexico-facing catalogue by that date, not the original session date or identity of the same master./1974 compilation.

### Ignacio Parra — narrative taxonomy and archive check (15 November 2026)

A further source check sharpens, but does not resolve, the thematic question:

- Jesús Iván Mora Muro, “El corrido en México: historiografía e instituciones,” in *Visiones históricas* (Universidad Autónoma de Querétaro, 2025), pp.123–125, says revenge (*venganza*) recurs as a leitmotif in nineteenth-century heroic corridos. He distinguishes the blood-feud bandit from the “social bandit” opposing exploitative elites, and places Ignacio Parra among the latter. This is genre-level and typological context, **not evidence that the 1904 cylinder itself narrates revenge**. The chapter's quoted narrative example is Macario Romero, not Parra.
- The same chapter's pp.235–236 independently lists the two Edison Parra parts as 18512–18513, both performed by Herrera Robinson, and says they were among the early corridos commercially issued from cylinders. The November 1907 *Edison Phonograph Monthly* Mexico-market catalogue separately lists “Ignacio Parra, 1a Parte” and “2a Parte” under Robinson. The 1907 list is a later catalogue witness, not a recording-session date; Frontera's account supplies the 1904 recording date.
- Agustín Gurza's Frontera essay characterizes *Ignacio Parra* and *Heraclio Bernal* as ballads about anti-Díaz rebels, and reports that Herrera did not reprise either in his later Victor/Columbia recordings. Gurza relays James Nicolopulos's interpretation that political pressure and a “seditious undercurrent” explain the omission. This supports a political-dissent reading of the repertory, but does not identify a specific retaliatory act in Parra's song.
- UCSB's Cylinder Audio Archive describes a public digital collection of more than 15,000 cylinders from a collection of over 22,000; its Todd Collection separately includes nearly 6,000 primarily commercial Edison/Columbia cylinders. The present search did **not** locate an item-level public record or audio for Edison 18512/18513. Absence from this search is not proof that UCSB lacks a copy. UCSB says catalogued recordings can be found through UC Library Search and digital copies may be requested through Special Research Collections.

**Taxonomy remains unresolved for the recorded performance.** Printed 1905/1914 versions that portray Parra's death as justice or society's vengeance must remain distinct from the 1904 cylinder until the cylinder is heard. Do not treat a corrido about an outlaw as automatically a revenge song, nor conflate officialist state punishment with the outlaw's retaliation or personal revenge. The recording is a **HIGH-PRIORITY LEAD / NEAR CANDIDATE**, not yet a candidate track and not cleared.

**Next archive actions:** ask UCSB Special Research Collections to search title variants “Ignacio Parra,” “Parra,” Edison 18512 and 18513 across the Todd Collection, card indexes and cylinder catalogue; ask whether any surviving cylinder has been digitized or can be made available for research. Then compare both sides with the separately catalogued print witnesses and the 1925 anthology, retaining variant readings.
