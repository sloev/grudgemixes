# Mardiros Der Sarkis Tashjian — 1909 Columbia Armenian-language recordings

**Status: LEAD / NOT CLEARED.** The metadata and a substantial lyric excerpt for “Haireniked Mortsir” are documented by the Armenian Museum of America. This is a promising early diaspora song about refusal to forget the homeland, but it is not a revenge song on the evidence currently available. “Himi el Lrenk” is a second, more explicitly silence/protest-oriented lead whose title translation is disputed.

## “Haireniked Mortsir” / “Hayrenikt Mortsir” — “Forget Your Homeland/Country”

- **Performer:** Mardiros Der Sarkis Tashjian (label/discography variants: M. D. S. Tashjian; M. O. S. Tashjian).
- **Language:** Armenian; the museum's quoted text is in Armenian script. Dialect/orthography should be confirmed with an Armenian-language specialist.
- **Recording:** early summer 1909, Columbia studio, New York City, according to Harout Arakelian's Armenian Museum of America research.
- **Issue:** Columbia E-531 / 4084; paired with “Last Rose of Summer,” Columbia E-531 / 1199. No matrix/take is established by the museum article or the catalogue evidence located in this pass.
- **Composition:** listed in early twentieth-century Armenian songbooks as “Hayrenaser” (“Patriot”), but the exact author/composer and the relationship between printed variants and this performance remain unverified. Composition gate therefore unresolved.
- **Lyrics/theme:** the museum provides an extended transcription and translation. The song stages an unnamed foreigner's instruction to forget the homeland because it is cursed, barren and ruined; the singer counters by praising the land and declaring enduring love for Armenia and Cilicia. The museum notes that this Columbia version opens differently from variants in printed songbooks.
- **Taxonomy:** **intergenerational/collective grievance; diasporic attachment; refusal of imposed forgetting** is possible as a contextual reading, but the excerpt primarily supports homeland attachment and resistance to assimilation, not a clearly articulated grievance mechanism. Do not classify as revenge, retaliation or spite.
- **Historical context:** the museum places the recording in the early summer of 1909, after news of the Adana massacres reached Armenian communities in America. It documents Nishan Tashjian's participation in a Boston protest meeting in August 1909, but its suggestion that discs may have raised humanitarian funds is explicitly conjectural. Do not claim this song was written in response to Adana or used for relief fundraising without independent evidence.

## “Himi el Lrenk” — provisional “Should We Still Remain Silent?”

- **Performer:** catalogued as M.O.S. Tashjian, tenor; the museum attributes the recording to Mardiros Der Sarkis Tashjian.
- **Language:** Armenian.
- **Recording:** Columbia E-series catalogue entry E-529, matrix 4100-1, recorded May 1909 in New York City. The catalogue's English title translation is **“Keep Silence for Awhile”**; the museum gives the title gloss **“Should We Still Remain Silent.”** These are not equivalent translations, and the original lyrics have not yet been verified against the sound recording.
- **Issue:** museum identifies Columbia E-529 / 4100; exact publication date and issue evidence still needed.
- **Taxonomy:** **possible protest / collective grievance testimony**, but do not classify until the lyrics and translation are independently checked. The title alone is insufficient.
- **Composition:** author/composer unknown in the evidence located here; composition gate unresolved.

## Related 1910 Tashjian Brothers repertoire

In autumn 1910, Mardiros, Nishan and Levon recorded 28 songs for Columbia in New York; the museum says these were released in 1911. Titles include “Kerezmanis Togh” / “Hrayr Tzhokhki Yerke” (song of Hrayr Tzhokhk), “Tashnagtsagan Khump” (Armenian Revolutionary Federation regiment), “Akh Vasburagan – Voghp Vasburagani” (lament for Vasburagan), “Yerp vor Patsvin” / “Giligia” (Cilicia), “Mer Hayrenik” (Our Fatherland), and “Azniv Enger” (Precious Friend). These are useful leads for local-language lyric research, but the museum's broad description as patriotic songs/odes to fedayi does not establish that any particular title is a grudge, revenge or retaliation song.

The Recording Pioneers Columbia E-Series matrix list independently corroborates the 1910 Tashjian Brothers session chronology and some matrix assignments, e.g. E-658 / matrix 4954-1, “Mir Heyrenik,” recorded October 1910 in NYC. It does not, in the material located, supply the missing 1909 “Haireniked Mortsir” matrix.
