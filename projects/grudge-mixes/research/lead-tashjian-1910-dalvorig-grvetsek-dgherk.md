# Tashjian Brothers 1910 Columbia repertoire: Dalvorig and Grvetsek Dgherk

**Status: LEADS / NOT CLEARED.** This note isolates two 1910 Tashjian Brothers titles with independently documented Armenian patriotic-song contexts. Neither is yet a verified revenge recording. The evidence currently supports a political/resistance repertoire and a need for lyric-level review.

## Recording evidence

Harout Arakelian's Armenian Museum of America discography states that Mardiros Der Sarkis Tashjian returned to Columbia in autumn 1910 with brothers Nishan and Levon; the trio recorded 28 Armenian-language songs, released in 1911. The museum gives the following title/issue associations:

- **“Dalvorig”** and **“Grvetsek Dgherk”** — Columbia E656 (paired sides; no individual matrix number shown in the article).
- **“Kerezmanis Togh”** — Columbia E657 / 4955; identified as “Hrayr Tzhokhki Yerke” (“Hrayr Tzhokhk's Song”).
- **“Tashnagtsagan Khump”** — Columbia E665 / 4962; “The Regiment of Armenian Revolutionary Federation.”
- **“I Piur Tzaynits”** — Columbia E665 / 4965.
- **“Akh Vasburagan”** — Columbia E666 / 4974; the paired “Zartir Vortiag” is E666 / 4975.
- **“Himi El Lrenk”** — Columbia E668 / 4953; paired with “I Zen Hayer,” E668 / 4967.

The museum says the brothers sang these as a three-part vocal group. Exact matrix/take data, original labels for all sides, issue advertisements and direct archival audio are not established here. The 1911 release year is reported by the museum; the date should be corroborated with contemporary catalogue/advertising evidence for each disc.

## “Dalvorig” and “Grvetsek Dgherk”: local patriotic-song context

Houshamadyan's account of the Capuchin college in Mezire (Mamuret-ul-Aziz/Harput region) says that after the 1908 Ottoman constitutional proclamation, the college orchestra played Armenian patriotic songs at public events, including **“Pamp Voradan,” “Grvetsek Dgherk” and “Dalvorig.”** This establishes that both titles circulated as patriotic repertoire in an Armenian institutional/public setting in the Harput region. It does not establish that the Tashjian recording derives from that exact local performance tradition, nor does it supply lyrics or authorship.

A 1962 Armenian Youth Federation of America anthology, *Armenian National Songs*, includes “Dalvorig” and “Grvetsek Dgherk” among a group of songs memorializing the Armenian Revolution and its heroes/events. Its introduction says these songs arose from popular traditions and that many were inadequately collated/classified. This is retrospective evidence of later repertoire classification, not proof of the date, authorship or precise text of the versions recorded by Tashjian.

## Taxonomy and historical caution

The museum characterizes the 1910 repertoire broadly as patriotic songs, odes to fedayi/freedom fighters and songs of loss. That does not automatically make any title revenge, retaliation or grievance testimony. In particular:

- “Grvetsek Dgherk” is commonly glossed “Take up arms, boys,” but the exact wording and performance text must be checked before interpreting it as a call to resistance or retaliation.
- “Dalvorig” is described by later Armenian-American commentary as a nationalistic song; its title and broad label do not establish a specific historical event or grievance mechanism.
- “Kerezmanis Togh” / “Hrayr Tzhokhki Yerke” may concern the fedayi figure Hrayr Tzhokhk, but a biographical/historical identification and the song's actual narrative need separate verification.
- The 1962 songbook includes “Kailerg Khanasori” (“March of Khanasor”) and describes the 1897 Khanasor expedition as an ARF-led attack against the Mazrig Kurdish tribe after an earlier ambush of Armenian fedayees. This is a potentially important comparative example of retaliatory collective violence in Armenian revolutionary song culture, but **Kailerg Khanasori is not among the Tashjian Brothers' 1910 Columbia titles listed by the museum**. Do not imply that it is their recording or transfer its narrative to “Dalvorig” or “Grvetsek Dgherk.”

## Composition, sound and rights gates

- The composer/lyricist and traditional provenance of the exact 1910 versions remain unknown.
- The 1962 anthology cannot by itself establish that a particular lyric or melody existed before 1926, nor identify the author/death date.
- No item-level raw archival transfer has been located. The museum's label images and discographical research are not a raw audio source.
- No commercial rights are cleared for master, composition, performance, transfer, digitization or territory.
