# Lead: Helen Paul, “Tzain ma Hunchetz” (Columbia E-4661; 1918)

**Status: LEAD — strong collective-resistance/protest content; exact master metadata, composition eligibility, raw audio and rights still open.**

## Identification from the surviving label
- Performer: Helen Paul, soprano.
- Title as rendered on the Columbia label: Armenian title with romanization “Tzanie Hentchetz” (the museum article uses “Tzain ma Hunchetz”; modern transliterations vary).
- Label/catalogue: Columbia E-4661.
- Label also prints “Solo-Violin Accomp.” and a parenthetical number “(84274)”; a runout inscription appears to read “84274,” but this needs a high-resolution inspection and discographical confirmation before treating it as a matrix.
- Recording: Armenian Museum of America says Helen Paul recorded in Columbia's New York studio at 233 Broadway, Woolworth Building, during an April 1918 session.
- Format: 78 rpm shellac disc, supported by the museum's original-label photograph and archive context.
- Direct label photograph: https://images.squarespace-cdn.com/content/v1/5963d44e03596ece54830d99/0c78bc03-de13-431a-8d5b-bc222f6b67fc/HelenPaulLabel.jpg
- Museum article: https://www.armenianmuseum.org/sound-archive/helen-paul

## The song and its text
The Armenian Museum of America identifies the piece as the first known recording of an ode to Armenian revolutionaries from Erzurum, especially the Protectors of the Fatherland (Bashdban Hayrenyats). It says the composition was written by an unknown author in the late nineteenth century. The museum describes the record as made in April 1918 and notes that the Nishkian Phonograph Shop in Fresno carried the Columbia disc.

A text under the Armenian title “Ձայն մը հնչեց” (“A sound rang out”) is available in Wikisource, headed “ՇԱՐԺՈՒՄ ՀԱՅՐԵՆԵԱՑ ԿԱՐՆՈՅ” (a movement/uprising of the fatherland of Karin/Erzurum):
- https://hy.wikisource.org/wiki/Ձայն_մը_հնչեց
- The text describes the call to arms among Armenians in Erzurum, villagers taking up swords and rifles, elders longing to see the homeland free, women urging men to fight, young people joining forces, and the Sultan hearing of the movement. Its closing stanzas celebrate unity, education, liberty and armed defence.
- The museum's title and description strongly suggest this is the song being discussed, but exact line-by-line identity between the Wikisource text and the recorded performance has not yet been established. Do not publish a transcription/translation as the record's lyrics until the performance or contemporaneous score is checked.

**Provisional taxonomy:** Collective grievance / revolutionary protest / armed resistance rhetoric. This is not a revenge song on the evidence reviewed. The lyric's political subject is organized resistance to imperial rule and a call for national liberty. Keep historical context and song rhetoric distinct; do not infer that the recording documents an actual battle or specific event.

## Composition and publication evidence
- The museum article says the text was composed by an unknown writer in the late 1800s. This suggests the composition predates the master, but an anonymous/unknown author does **not automatically satisfy** the project's composition gate. Need evidence that the work is a traditional song with provenance, or identify an author whose death predates 1956.
- The museum says a version of the sheet music appeared in *Songs of Armenia*, edited by C. H. Paelian and arranged by K. H. Aiqouni, published in 1924. A separately digitized 1919 *Songs of Armenia* is available at https://archives.webaram.com/dvdk_new/eng/songs-of-armenia-1919_OCR.pdf, but this pass did not verify whether it contains this exact piece.
- The April 1918 session date is supported by the museum's item-specific research. Exact Columbia catalogue issue date and matrix assignment remain to be confirmed. The label catalogue E-4661 and parenthetical/runout number 84274 provide a concrete path for checking Columbia catalogues and matrix ledgers.

## Editorial assessment
This is potentially one of the strongest early Armenian-American collective-resistance recordings located so far. Its significance is not that it is a revenge song, but that a commercial diaspora disc captured a late-nineteenth-century revolutionary ode in New York in April 1918, just weeks before the declaration of the First Republic of Armenia. It may be suitable for a collective grievance / resistance sequence if the exact recorded lyrics and composition status can be established.
