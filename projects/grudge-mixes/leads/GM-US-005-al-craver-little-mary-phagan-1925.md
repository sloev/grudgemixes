# Al Craver [Vernon Dalhart] — “Little Mary Phagan” (Columbia 15031-D, 1925)

**Project ID:** GM-US-005  
**Status:** C — LEAD / exact commercial issue and matrix identified; thematic and ethical review required  
**Core date:** PROBABLE DATE — Columbia matrix listing gives 27 May 1925; Smithsonian confirms the 1925 shellac issue.  
**Proposed taxonomy:** Public accusation; collective grievance mobilization; contested/miscarried justice.  
**Editorial caution:** This is not a neutral murder ballad. It participates in a public campaign that helped mobilize hostility against Leo Frank, a Jewish man whose conviction is now widely regarded by historians as a miscarriage of justice shaped by antisemitism. Do not include without explicit editorial framing and review.

## Historical and thematic context

Mary Phagan, age thirteen, was murdered in Atlanta in 1913. Leo Frank, superintendent at the pencil factory where she worked, was convicted in a highly controversial trial and sentenced to death. Governor John M. Slaton commuted the sentence; Frank was kidnapped from prison and lynched in August 1915. The Georgia Historical Society's historical marker records the lynching and the 1986 posthumous pardon, which acknowledged the state's failure to protect Frank and bring his killers to justice without formally adjudicating guilt or innocence: https://www.georgiahistory.com/ghmi_marker_updated/leo-frank-lynching/

Historian Leonard Dinnerstein's New Georgia Encyclopedia account describes the case as a miscarriage of justice and documents how antisemitism, labor resentment and political agitation shaped public opinion: https://www.georgiaencyclopedia.org/articles/history-archaeology/leo-frank-case/ . The Encyclopedia's overview of Jews in Georgia says scholars generally agree Frank was almost certainly innocent: https://www.georgiaencyclopedia.org/articles/arts-culture/judaism-and-jews/

John Carson's earlier “Little Mary Phagan” ballad was performed publicly during the campaign for Frank's execution. The New Georgia Encyclopedia states that the song proclaimed Frank's guilt and reflected the sentiments of many Georgians; it also notes that Frank is now widely regarded as an innocent victim of antisemitism: https://www.georgiaencyclopedia.org/articles/arts-culture/fiddlin-john-carson-ca-1868-1949/ . Tony Russell's *Rural Rhythm* chapter identifies the Columbia 15031-D recording as Al Craver [Vernon Dalhart]: https://academic.oup.com/book/39669/chapter-abstract/339656506

This is relevant to a grievance archive not as proof of the accusation but as an example of a commercial ballad circulating a confident public accusation and helping transform a contested legal case into collective outrage. The song's moral framing and the historical truth of the case must be kept separate. Its grievance mechanism may be accusation, public shaming and mobilization; it must not be presented as a justified response to an established crime.

## Textual evidence and limitations

A 1918 text witness published by Franklyn Bliss Snyder in the *Journal of American Folk-Lore* is reproduced here: https://famous-trials.com/leo-frank/38-ballad . It is a historical text witness for the broader ballad tradition, not a transcript of Dalhart's 1925 master. Carson's versions changed over time; the lyrics in the Columbia recording must be transcribed from that exact performance before any quotation or detailed thematic claim is attributed to it.
