# Peg Leg Howell and Jim Hill — “Ball and Chain Blues” (Columbia 14535-D, 1929)

**Project ID:** GM-US-010  
**Status:** B — PROBABLE GRIEVANCE RECORDING / strong prison-labor complaint in published lyric witness; exact master still needs transcription  
**Session:** Atlanta, 13 April 1929 (specialist discography)  
**Matrix:** 148270-2  
**Issue:** Columbia 14535-D  
**Performers:** Peg Leg Howell, vocal and guitar; Jim Hill, mandolin.

## Exact recording identity

The Mainspring / Document Records booklet for *The Stringband Era* identifies “Ball and Chain Blues,” recording number 148270-2, by Peg Leg Howell and Jim Hill, at Atlanta on 13 April 1929. https://img1.wsimg.com/blobby/go/fac2e2b3-4447-46cd-9620-8ac813731e52/MSESET1%20digital%20booklet-0001.pdf

The illustrated Peg Leg Howell discography also identifies the title and matrix 148270-2, paired with “Away From Home,” Columbia 14535-D, recorded 13 April 1929. https://wirz.de/music/howell.htm

One secondary artist page gives 8 April 1927 for the same matrix/title, which conflicts with the 1929 sessionography and is evidently a date error; preserve the discrepancy rather than repeating it. https://mail.violafair.com/hmrproject/h/howell-pegleg.htm

## Thematic evidence

A transcribed lyric witness attributed to Howell's 1929 recording describes arrest, a judge who will not let the narrator speak, an excessive or unjust sentence, prison labour, stripes on the back and a ball and chain. This is strong evidence of complaint about criminal process and penal labour, though the transcription is a community-posted text and must be checked against the exact master before quotation or final grading. https://mudcat.org/thread.cfm?threadid=157360

The Library of Congress explains that African American prison work songs and blues often complained about prison conditions and labour; some explicitly voiced escape or false accusation. This provides historical context, not proof of Howell's personal circumstances or the exact song's source. https://www.loc.gov/collections/songs-of-america/articles-and-essays/historical-topics/blues-as-protest/

Peg Leg Howell himself recalled being imprisoned in 1925 for selling whiskey and said he learned “New Prison Blues” while incarcerated. That biographical fact should not be collapsed into “Ball and Chain Blues”: the song's narrator and the singer are not automatically identical. Interview/biographical material: https://sundayblues.org/wp-content/uploads/2011/02/PegLegHowell.pdf

## Thematic assessment

The strongest provisional reading is a first-person grievance against punitive justice and forced prison labour: denial of a chance to speak, harsh sentencing, degrading stripes and chained labour. This is more directly relevant to grievance/protest than an outlaw title alone. Grade B is provisional because the lyric witness needs verification against the recording and the exact issue/take.
