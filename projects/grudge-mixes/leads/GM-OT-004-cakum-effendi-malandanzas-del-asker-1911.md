# GM-OT-004 — Çakum Effendi, “Malandanzas del asker” (Gramophone 11-12734, 1911)

## Identification

- Performer: Çakum Effendi, a Salonica-based Sephardic singer.
- Judeo-Spanish (Ladino) title: “Malandanzas del asker” (“Hardships / misfortunes of the soldier”).
- Original 78 label title / incipit: “Avre tus ojos i mira” (“Open your eyes and look”).
- Label / catalogue: Gramophone 11-12734.
- Recording session: Salonica, 1911. The Hebrew University / Jewish Music Research Centre booklet says Çakum Effendi's eight Judeo-Spanish songs and two liturgical pieces in the EMI Archive Trust collection were all recorded in 1911.
- Matrix: not supplied in the scholarly booklet; do not infer one from catalogue number.
- Language: Judeo-Spanish (Ladino).
- Duration: 2:59 in the 2020 scholarly anthology (some streaming listings round to 2:58).
- Access: included as track 42 on CD 2 of *Eastern Mediterranean Judeo-Spanish Songs from the EMI Archive Trust (1907–1912)* (2020). Apple Music and Amazon Music list the track. No freely downloadable original archive transfer established in this pass.

## Text and thematic evidence

The Hebrew University scholarly commentary identifies this as one of the Ladino songs about “askerlik” — military service imposed on Jews by Ottoman authorities from 1908. It notes that Çakum's version is close to Haim Effendi's earlier / parallel recorded version and that rare oral-tradition versions probably derive from these commercial recordings.

The scholarly booklet transcribes the Çakum recording. The opening reads:

- “Ah, abre tus ojos y mira / lo que vino al mundo” — “Ah, open your eyes and look / at what has come into the world.”
- “El hijo ya se hue al asker / yo v’a salir del mundo” — “The son has gone off to military service / I am going to leave this world.”

The following verses stage a family farewell: a mother asks why she is suffering, and the son asks her to look at him as he departs with the caravan. The song's emotional centre is not battlefield heroism but the cost of conscription for the family left behind.

The song is a strong collective-grievance / coercive-state-service candidate. It should not be over-described as an organized anti-conscription protest: the recorded text is a lament, not a manifesto, and its precise speaker/character changes should be assessed from the full performance. The 1908 policy context is the scholarly commentary's framing; distinguish the beginning of the policy from the 1911 recording date.
