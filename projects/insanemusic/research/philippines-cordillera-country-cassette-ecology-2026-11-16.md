# Cordillera vernacular country / local-language cassette ecology

**Research date:** 2026-11-16  
**Status:** ENVIRONMENT / RECORDING LEADS — not a new catalogue ID yet  
**Region:** Baguio City, Benguet and wider Cordillera, northern Luzon, Philippines  
**Languages represented in the research:** Kankana-ey, Ibaloi, Ilokano, plus English  
**Direction:** Non-prison, non-protest community music; local-language songwriting, community radio, regional country/pop, local studios and small-scale cassette circulation

## Why this is a separate research direction

The Cordillera's recorded music history should not be reduced to DKK / Salidummay and political movement music. A distinct local country/pop ecosystem developed around Baguio and Benguet, with local-language lyrics, community radio, homegrown songwriters, makeshift recording studios, cassette and later CD/video distribution, and performance spaces. This is a contemporary musical culture made by local artists who used modern media to represent their own experiences. It is not a generic “traditional music” category and should not be described as a survival of an unchanged past.

Jimmy Balud Fong's 2007 paper, *Batawa: Constructing Identity through Country Music in the Philippine Cordillera*, explicitly argues that contemporary local-language songs are cultural products through which Igorot makers represent who they are and what they have become. It describes the 1970s local Ibaloy country song wave, then a 1990s resurgence in cassette recordings using more local languages and varied themes. Some artists reportedly became recognised singers in their communities only after albums appeared; some recordings were made in makeshift studios in Baguio rather than Manila. Fong discusses the song “Benguet Country” by RJ Mero and the video album *Ivadoy Jen Country Songs*, including songs popularised by Cesar Pasiw, and the video CD *Kabenguet an* by Rio Felimon Cariño and D' Friends Band.

Fong's paper is valuable as a first-hand scholarly map of the scene and names concrete recordings and makers. However, it does not by itself provide the full cassette discography, catalogue numbers, release dates or original audio for every example. The examples below therefore remain leads until their physical or authorised digital recording objects are matched.

## Kinnoboyan — a specific recording cluster

Gretchen Shagami C. Mangahas' study, *Indigenous Songs: An Intercultural Communication Strategy for Social Values Development Among the Youth of Benguet, Philippines*, describes the Kinnoboyan band as emerging from the Mountain Province Broadcasting Corporation's 2006 “Magic Countryhan” search for local folk and country singers. Five finalists were gathered into the band:
- Junie Rey Bag-ayan
- Heintje Pis-oy
- Gelasio Beray
- Stephen Lael Senio
- Christopher Longalong

The study says three members were Kankana-ey and two Ibaloi, and that the band composed twelve songs: eight Kankana-ey, three Ibaloi and one English. It lists titles including “An-anusak,” “San Biyag,” “Pangaasim Koma,” “Tsismosa,” “Bay-am Kod Pay,” “Linmingling Ka,” “Kimat,” “Ed Ili,” “834,” “No Ayshi Ha,” “Seseskeren Taha” and one English song. The study reports the band's popularity through local radio requests and frames its songs as original compositions about personal experiences and lessons in life. This is a more specific, non-protest community music case than a generic regional genre description.

### Concrete release to investigate: *Inlove With You* (2014)

Apple Music lists *Inlove With You* by Junie Rey Bag-Ayan and Heintje Pis-Oy, released 6 October 2014, with 13 tracks and a displayed duration of 59 minutes. Qobuz gives a 59:09 duration and credits Junie Rey Bag-Ayan as label / main artist, with Heintje Pis-Oy also a main artist. Its tracklist includes “An-Anusak,” “Halsema Highway,” “Tsismosa,” “Ed Ili,” and “Nu Ayshi Ha,” titles that overlap with the Kinnoboyan repertoire listed in the Mangahas study. This is an important possible link between the 2006 Kinnoboyan song corpus and a later duo release by two of its members.

**Do not assume the 2014 album is the Kinnoboyan band's 12-song album, a cassette reissue, or the same recordings heard in the 2006 study.** The album has 13 tracks, the credits are for a duo, and no item-level liner notes or master comparison has been found. It is a digital release route; no physical cassette has been established.

Apple Music separately lists Junie Rey Bag-Ayan's “An-Anusak” as a 2009 single. This could be an earlier issue of one of the Kinnoboyan songs, but no master/version comparison has been made. A current platform listing is not evidence that the recording was originally a cassette.

## Other named Cordillera recording leads

- **“Benguet Country” — RJ Mero.** Named by Fong as a song whose refrain describes local country music. No matched album, release date, label or authorised audio source identified yet.
- **Rio Felimon Cariño and D' Friends Band — *Kabenguet an*.** Fong identifies this as a video CD, describing it as a musical mapping/claiming of Benguet places, with location footage around the province. The source gives a named recording object and makers but not an exact issue date or full tracklist.
- **Cesar Pasiw — *Ivadoy Jen Country Songs*.** Fong describes this as a video album featuring songs popularised by Pasiw, including “Savong Shi Bahong” and “Nonta Panagbenga.” No verified physical copy or full release metadata located.
- **“Batawa.”** Fong says this popular song was recorded by several local artists and gave its name to a Baguio country-music bar. The title alone is not enough to identify one performance or master.
- **MPBC Kinnoboyan Band / Kinnoboyan Originals, Inc.** Further local radio and live-performance evidence appears in Fong and Mangahas. Keep the contest band, the later Kinnoboyan Originals organisation, and individual artist recordings distinct unless the sources explicitly link them.

## Small-web and community source

The 2006 Igorot blog *From the Boondocks* posted “Benguet Country Singer,” identifying the singer in the video as Rio “Filemon” Cariño and inviting local readers to translate the song. A comment says the producer was Cariño and company; the blogger notes that the group had other videos on YouTube. The discussion includes community members recognising the artist and requesting further local videos. This is useful small-web reception evidence and a possible route to the producers, but it does not identify a cassette or establish the video's recording date. ## Archive routes — contextual, not catalogue entries

### University of the Philippines Center for Ethnomusicology (UPCE)

UPCE's official facilities page reports that, as of 2014, its unpublished collection contained 2,424 open reels, 191 cassettes and about 20 digital audio items documenting traditional music from about 87 ethnolinguistic groups in the Philippines, Asia and elsewhere. Its published collection then included 610 vinyl records, 500 cassettes and 332 CDs. UPCE says archive materials can be accessed in person or online by request to **upethno_archive.upd@up.edu.ph**. Its online access is subject to the archive's own conditions; this is not a blanket permission to download or reuse recordings.

This is a major route for future non-Anglo research, but the figures are an institutional collection overview, not a catalogue-level confirmation of any particular recording. The next step is to search the UPCE online catalogue and request a reference interview for named Cordillera/Ibaloy/Kankana-ey material.

### Library of Congress — *The Mountains Ring Out Their Joy* (1987)

The American Folklife Center's Philippines guide identifies AFC 1996/028, the Ron Walcott / Philippine and Sri Lankan Music Collection, as containing one cassette titled *The Mountains Ring Out Their Joy*: 25 Christian songs from the Cordilleras of northern Philippines, sung mostly a cappella, recorded in the Philippines by Ron Wolcott and produced by the Asian Institute for Liturgy and Music in 1987. The guide also describes an accompanying 42-page songbook with musical and lyric transcriptions.

This is a specific, extant cassette and an important local-language/community singing source. It is not automatically an Insane Music catalogue item: the current evidence describes a produced Christian music collection rather than an outsider, self-organised or marginal recording practice. Keep it as a contextual/archive lead unless further evidence establishes a more specific community setting, named makers, unusual circulation or other project-relevant pathway.

### UP Diliman Digital Archiving of Linguistic Fieldwork Cassette Tapes (DALFCT)

The UP Department of Linguistics project reports a 749-cassette fieldwork archive, with material across numerous Philippine languages including Kankana-ey, Ibaloi, Ifugao, Kalinga, Kankanaey, Ilokano, Sama, Tausug, Teduray, Tinggian and others. It aims to digitise the tapes, create a digital language-data archive and eventually repatriate materials to source communities, subject to ethical clearance. This is an archival research route, not a music discography: much of the collection is linguistic fieldwork, and music should not be presumed present merely because a language is represented. Ask for catalogue-level descriptions and ethical/access conditions before treating any item as a music source.

## Follow-up archive research — Sagada community archive and ReCollection (2026-11-16)

### A. Collection and ReCollection: a community-controlled 2025 sound archive

UP Center for Ethnomusicology (UPCE) published a detailed account of “Collection and ReCollection: recording the traditional vocal and instrumental repertoire in Sagada.” Sagada is described as a Kankana-ey community in Mountain Province. The project ran 11 February–30 June 2025 as a partnership between UPCE and Saint Mary’s School of Sagada, Inc. (SMSSI), building on the UPCE relationship with the community since Jose Maceda’s 1958 research and later 2013–2020 repatriation and documentation work.

The important distinction is governance: UPCE reports that community members made the decisions about schedule, participants, what to record and preserve, and whom to interview; the stated principle is that community members record themselves and retain full control over the project and its outcomes. Thirty-seven participants worked on data gathering and metadata processing. The project compiled 200 recordings: 167 audio files and 33 video files. Reported content includes interviews, chants, songs and instrumental performances by resource persons and elders, plus recordings made during weddings, a funeral and a gubbaw (naming ceremony).

Access and custody are explicit: original recordings remain with SMSSI; UPCE received an off-site copy on 19 May 2025, available for room use only. Copies were also to be furnished to the National Commission for Culture and the Arts and the National Archives of the Philippines under the grant. This is not an open-download collection, and the community's control and ethical decisions take precedence over the researcher's convenience.

Project relevance: this is an unusually clear example of a locally governed, living community sound archive and a practical model for respectful documentation. It is not automatically an Insane Music entry: the described repertoire is community cultural practice, not evidence of outsider, DIY or marginal musical status. Retain as a methodological/archive route and only pursue item-level material if the community and archive consider access appropriate.

### B. ReCollection / older field recordings in Sagada and Kabayan

UPCE's 2021 account of its ReCollection programme says that digital copies of Jose Maceda Collection materials dating from as early as 1953 were repatriated to Sagada and Mountain Province local government in March 2013. The programme combines revisits to research communities, repatriation of digital copies, and checking whether older recorded music remains known or practiced.

The same account identifies 1987 recordings made by Ramon P. Santos at the Bendian Festival in Kabayan, Benguet. Copies were repatriated during a 2017 programme alongside the launch of The Vocal Repertoire from Kabayan. Elders recognised voices in excerpts played over a loudspeaker. The account also describes field recordings from the 1950s being returned to Sagada in 2020 and local collaborator Alma Louise Bagano transcribing and translating the texts.

These are concrete dated recording corpora, but the public account does not supply item-level catalogue numbers, performers, titles, carrier identifiers or a track-level access route. They belong in the archive/contextual register until catalogue-level descriptions can identify specific recordings and their circumstances. Do not infer that the 1950s materials were made by residents or that the 1987 Bendian recordings qualify as outsider music.

### Exact access route and next steps

UPCE's facilities page states that archive materials can be accessed in person or online by request to upethno_archive.upd@up.edu.ph; it also describes room-use restrictions and the Center's reference-interview service. For the 2025 Sagada archive, UPCE says its copy is room-use only and the originals remain with SMSSI. The appropriate first step is a narrowly scoped catalogue/reference enquiry, not a request for unrestricted copies: ask whether item-level metadata for the 1987 Kabayan recordings, the 1950s Sagada corpus and any earlier Kankana-ey/Ibaloy field recordings can be shared, who controls access, and whether community review is required. For the 2025 collection, approach the community archive through its stated governance route and do not presume UPCE can authorise access to originals.

## 2026-11-16 follow-up — Abatan Records and local cassette infrastructure

A further source pass identified a more concrete local-label route beyond the previously mapped artists:

- Maurice Malanes, “In Cordillera, resilience vs piracy,” Philippine Daily Inquirer (12 March 2013), reports that Elsie Gawidan Maliones established Abatan Records in the 1970s. The article describes her accompanying local Kankanaey and Ibaloy singers to a Manila recording studio after their songs had become popular locally, and her sale of originally recorded cassette tapes in Baguio during the 1990s. This is contemporary reporting about a named local label/producer and a documented original-cassette distribution chain; it is not an item-level discography and does not establish any particular tape's surviving audio.
- Jimmy Balud Fong's Cordillera country-music study is also indexed in the 2007 INTER conference proceedings. The full text names Ivadoy Jen Country Songs, says it features songs popularised by Cesar Pasiw, and identifies “Savong Shi Bahong” and “Nonta Panagbenga”; it also discusses Rio Felimon Cariño and D' Friends Band's video CD Kabenguetan. This strengthens the title/artist/context crosswalk but does not supply catalogue numbers, original carrier scans or an authenticated audio master.
- A 2024 local Igorot Singers Gallery profile describes Rio Cariño as an Ibaloi-Igorot singer/songwriter/producer and refers to his 1980s album production. A 2018 Philippine News Agency report documents the Ivadoy language's continuing use in popular compositions and music videos. These are contextual corroboration only, not proof of a particular surviving cassette.
- A 2013 newspaper account and the later producer profile must not be used to treat local-language country/pop as outsider music automatically. No new IM-ID is justified: a specific extant recording, maker/context pathway, release identity and provenance still need item-level verification. Kabenguetan is described as a 2004 VCD, not an early cassette; it is useful as a later scene/infrastructure reference, not a cassette candidate.

### Next concrete route
Seek an itemised Abatan Records catalogue, original cassette sleeves/labels and tracklists, and a legitimate archive/owner route for one named Kankanaey/Ibaloy recording. Prioritise the 1990s original cassette editions and distinguish them from later bootleg CDs, remixes, video CDs and digital platform uploads. Ask for metadata and permission conditions before requesting copies. Do not contact anyone or infer permission from public availability.

### What this does — and does not — establish

These are item-level field-recording objects from Bontok/Sadanga in 1983. They are **not** the 1987 Ramon P. Santos Bendian Festival recordings from Kabayan, Benguet, nor the 1950s Sagada corpus. The catalogue's 1987 Kalinga results surfaced in this search were photographs (for example UPCE-P-8646, a slide of men playing gangsa), not the Kabayan audio objects. Do not merge Kalinga photo records, the 1983 Bontok cassettes, the 1987 Kabayan recordings or the 2025 SMSSI community archive.

No specific outsider, DIY, psychiatric, institutional, closed-community or alternative-art pathway is established for the 1983 Bontok field recordings. They document a local musical tradition; unfamiliarity or Indigenous identity is not an outsider qualification. They remain a **contextual archive finding**, not a main-discography entry, and no IM-ID is assigned.
