# Research method and collaboration protocol

**Project:** *Insane Music* — a worldwide discography and archival map of outsider, unconventional, self-taught, underground and institutional music, including documented psychiatric histories.
**Status:** Expanded scope, 15 November 2026. The project title is intentionally provocative; it is not a clinical judgement or a label imposed on contributors.

## 1. Mission and editorial principle

Collect and document recordings by people and groups whose musical lives or practices sit outside dominant musical institutions, conventions or markets, alongside music made in psychiatric institutions and by people with documented psychiatric histories.

**We collect the music, not the diagnoses. We document the lives, not reduce people to labels.**

“Outsider,” “unconventional,” “DIY,” “self-taught,” “underground” and “experimental” describe different things. They are not synonyms for mental illness, disability, social exclusion or lack of musical ability. Use the most precise description supported by evidence. Do not call a person a “freak,” “weirdo” or “mad” in editorial prose unless quoting, contextualising a historical source, or documenting a person's own chosen self-description. The project's title does not license disrespectful language.

The project is global. Do not treat North American and Western European scenes as the default, or assume that a lack of English-language documentation means a scene or artist does not exist. Search local languages, scripts, labels, community archives, oral histories and regional networks.

## 2. Main admission rule: an extant recording

The main discography requires an identifiable, existing sound recording of music. It may be:
- privately held, unpublished, unreleased or inaccessible online;
- issued commercially or independently;
- self-recorded, home-recorded, field-recorded, live, broadcast or institutionally recorded;
- analogue or digital: wax cylinder, piano roll with captured performance, shellac/78, lacquer or acetate, vinyl, wire, open-reel tape, 8-track, compact cassette, DAT, CD, VHS or other video sound, hard drive, born-digital file, web-native recording;
- a complete work, track, fragment, demo, rehearsal, improvisation or documented excerpt.

A catalogue description, photograph of a musician, written score, concert programme or recollection alone does not prove that a sound recording survives. Record such material in a linked **contextual register**, not as an audio item. A recording need not be publicly streamable, but its existence and provenance must be documented. Do not imply that you heard a recording if you have only read its catalogue description.

## 3. Who and what qualifies: overlapping pathways

An artist or group may qualify through one or more of these pathways. Record the exact pathway(s); do not collapse them into a single identity.

### A. Institutional music
Music made, performed, composed or recorded in a psychiatric hospital, asylum, sanatorium, psychiatric ward, long-stay psychiatric institution or other clearly documented mental-health setting. Includes patient ensembles, workshops, choirs, music therapy/occupational activity, ward recordings and individual patient recordings.

### B. Documented psychiatric history
A musician with a credible, independently documented psychiatric history, whether or not they were institutionalised and regardless of where the recording was made. A diagnosis is not required if the sources document hospitalisation or another relevant psychiatric experience; describe exactly what is documented and do not infer more.

### C. Self-taught and autodidactic practice
Musicians whose practice developed substantially outside formal musical training or conventional professional systems, where that independence is relevant to the work's history. “Self-taught” is not a synonym for unskilled or unsophisticated.

### D. DIY, home-recorded and self-released music
Artists who made, duplicated, distributed or archived recordings independently, especially personal cassette catalogues, homemade LPs/CDRs, private pressings, mail-order releases, bedroom recordings, one-person labels, hand-distributed tapes and other non-industrial circulation. DIY status alone is a discovery pathway, not automatic proof of outsider significance: document the particular context and avoid admitting every independent release indiscriminately.

### E. Outsider, vernacular and idiosyncratic musical practice
Distinctive individual or collective musical practices developed outside dominant professional, academic or commercial norms. This can include unusual instrumentation, personal musical systems, idiosyncratic song forms, private musical worlds, vernacular invention, and work that resists conventional genre classification. The music's difference is a reason to investigate, not evidence of a diagnosis or a person's capacity.

### F. Marginal and underground scenes
Recordings from poorly documented, locally marginalised, informal, underground, subcultural or geographically overlooked scenes, where the recording history or circulation is relevant to the project's outsider/independent remit. Include local context and avoid treating an entire culture or non-Western musical tradition as “outsider” simply because it is unfamiliar to Western listeners.

### G. Art environments and alternative communities
Music made within documented outsider-art communities, alternative living environments, community workshops, disability-led arts settings, prison or other closed institutions, and comparable settings. Explain the specific relationship and do not conflate different kinds of institution or experience.

### H. Unwanted music (strand added 2026-11-20)
Music that was rejected, ridiculed or used as a nuisance, where the spite relationship is documented. Three branches: (1) **rough music**, the noise rituals of charivari, *cencerrada*, *scampanata* and *Katzenmusik* used to shame an offender; (2) **music as nuisance**, music engineered or deployed to annoy or repel; (3) **unwanted voices**, makers whose music the culture laughed at or ignored and who made it anyway. The joke is on taste, never on the maker. Famous anchors are context only (§3B). Strangeness alone is never enough (§4). Rough-music recordings must protect private targets. Full strand note: `research/unwanted-strand-annoying-and-weirdo-music-2026-11-20.md`.

### Admission threshold
For a main-discography entry, require:
1. evidence that the recording exists;
2. an identifiable artist, group, credited alias or responsibly described anonymous source;
3. at least one clearly documented pathway above, with evidence explaining why it is relevant;
4. enough provenance to distinguish the item from rumour, misattribution or a later reconstruction.

For broad pathways C–F, require a concrete contextual case, not merely an unusual sound, outsider marketing, a collector's adjective, an eccentric image, low sales, or lack of formal training in isolation. Multiple independent contextual indicators are preferable. The editorial team may admit a compelling single-person archive or self-described outsider practice where the evidence is direct and the musical-historical case is clear.

Do not impose a psychiatric diagnosis as a gate for outsider music. Do not use psychiatric status as a shortcut for musical value.


## 3A. Environment-first discovery map

The primary research strategy is to investigate environments and collections where unusual, overlooked, self-organised or institutionally situated music may have been made, preserved or circulated. Do not begin only with lists of psychiatric diagnoses or famous outsider artists. Search the place/community/institution first, then identify the recording, its makers, circumstances and provenance. These environments are discovery routes, not automatic inclusion categories.

Maintain a multi-tag environment map:
1. Psychiatric hospitals, asylums, sanatoria, wards and therapeutic institutions.
2. Prisons, jails, penal colonies, detention centres and prison camps.
3. Closed religious communities, sects, new religious movements and communal worship groups.
4. Intentional communities, communes, utopian settlements and alternative societies.
5. Wartime camps, internment, forced-labour systems, concentration camps, gulags and refugee camps.
6. Orphanages, residential schools, reformatories, boarding schools and youth institutions.
7. Remote/isolated environments: islands, lighthouses, ships, polar stations, mining camps, frontier settlements and remote work sites.
8. Outsider-art workshops, disability-led arts settings, self-taught artist communities and alternative cultural spaces.
9. Underground circulation networks: cassette labels, private pressings, mail-order tapes, pirate radio, home studios and anonymous/pseudonymous releases.
10. Esoteric, spiritualist, occult, ceremonial and experimental religious settings.

Also search military barracks, hospitals outside psychiatry, sanatoriums, leprosy colonies, rehabilitation centres, workhouses, factories, ships, migrant/refugee hostels, community radio stations, language/cultural preservation projects and other closed or highly structured settings where a specific musical archive may exist.

For every environment, distinguish:
- music created by residents/members/inmates;
- music performed for them by outsiders or staff;
- music broadcast or played inside the environment;
- recordings made there but created elsewhere;
- recordings recovered there after closure, dispersal or a disaster;
- later recordings about or inspired by the environment.

These are not interchangeable. A tape found in a prison is not necessarily prison-made music; a song performed in a camp after liberation is not an original camp recording; a religious album sold by a group is not necessarily recorded inside its commune.

### Environment-first search workflow
For each site/community:
1. Identify its local and historic names, dates, geography, languages, operators and successor archive.
2. Search institutional archives, national libraries, radio collections, local newspapers, community histories, catalogues, finding aids, oral histories and private collections.
3. Search for musical infrastructure: choirs, bands, instruments, chapels, recreation halls, concerts, dances, music lessons, therapy/workshops, radio rooms, recording equipment, tape libraries and labels.
4. Search for media/provenance clues: cylinders, discs, wire, reels, cassettes, acetates, broadcast logs, sound films, home videos and digital files.
5. Trace named performers, recordists, staff, visiting musicians, local collectors and later custodians.
6. Verify whether the actual audio survives; distinguish original recording from later performance, reconstruction, reissue, dub or documentary soundtrack.
7. Assess consent, coercion, privacy, power relations, collecting history and rights before publication.
8. Log sources searched, including null results and unresolved questions.

### Special ethical caution
Cults, prisons, forced-labour and concentration camps, detention sites, residential schools and psychiatric institutions involve very different histories and power relations. Do not flatten them into a single sensational category. Use historically accurate names, foreground makers' agency where evidence allows, and identify coercion, exploitation, violence and contested institutional accounts honestly. Do not aestheticise suffering or present music as proof that a community was benign or that its members shared one belief.



## 3B. Obscurity and marginality threshold

The project prioritises **marginal, poorly circulated and genuinely overlooked recordings**. Obscurity is a selection criterion, not a substitute for provenance or musical relevance. A famous musician, notorious case, heavily documented cult, or widely circulated prison recording is not a priority merely because its backstory is unusual.

Before promoting a lead to the main research queue, assess:
1. **Maker marginality:** was the maker/group outside dominant music-industry, institutional or cultural channels, or overlooked in its own local context?
2. **Recording obscurity:** is this a scarce/private/unreleased/local/anonymous/poorly catalogued recording, or one known mainly to a small specialist circle?
3. **Environment specificity:** is the connection to the institution/community real and documented, rather than a sensational anecdote or incidental association?
4. **Discovery value:** would a curious researcher likely have missed it without investigating that environment, archive or local network?
5. **Evidence quality:** can the recording, maker and provenance be verified?

Use an editorial visibility rating: **HIGH PRIORITY** (obscure and strongly evidenced); **PROMISING** (obscure but one or more facts remain unverified); **CONTEXT ONLY** (valuable background but already well circulated or not sufficiently marginal); **DO NOT PRIORITISE** (notorious/famous, weakly connected, or not a recording). Do not confuse obscurity with artistic merit, and do not hide a recording's importance simply because it later became better known. Record the basis for the rating and revisit it if new evidence changes the picture.

Examples of calibration: a little-known ensemble's surviving home tapes from a small commune may be high priority; an internationally Grammy-nominated prison project may be context only; a notorious individual's widely bootlegged recordings are not a priority. The threshold is about the project's discovery mission, not a judgement on the people involved.

## 4. Exclusions and cautions

Do not admit an item solely because:
- it sounds strange, primitive, childish, noisy, dissonant, amateurish, disturbing or “crazy” to a listener;
- the musician has a disability, unusual appearance, poverty, homelessness, criminal record, marginalised identity or unconventional lifestyle;
- a journalist, label, collector or seller markets the artist as “mad,” “freak,” “primitive,” “naïve” or “outsider” without substantiation;
- the artist is simply unpopular, obscure, independent or experimental, with no meaningful connection to the project's defined scope.

These may prompt research, but they are not proof. Do not diagnose by listening or image. Do not presume that a person's music expresses symptoms, that their work is therapeutic, or that psychiatric history explains their art.

## 5. Classification and confidence

Use multiple tags, not one exclusive genre:
- **Institutional:** made in institution; made during residence; recorded/collected in institution; institutional group activity.
- **Biography/context:** documented psychiatric history; self-described outsider; self-taught; DIY/home-recorded; independent/self-released; underground scene; outsider-art/community setting; other evidenced context.
- **Recording:** cylinder; disc; wire; reel; cassette; cartridge; vinyl; CD; video; digital; broadcast; field recording; private archive.
- **Geography:** country/region of artist, recording, institution, label and archive separately where they differ.

Evidence status:
- **VERIFIED:** the recording's existence and core identity/pathway are supported by strong, mutually consistent evidence.
- **PARTIALLY VERIFIED:** a recording or archive object is established, but important metadata, identity, context or provenance remains uncertain.
- **LEAD:** plausible discovery requiring verification.
- **CONTEXT:** relevant history or source collection, but not yet a specific recording.
- **REJECTED / OUT OF SCOPE:** explain why; retain the reason to prevent rediscovery.

Do not upgrade a claim because several websites repeat the same unsourced text. Trace claims back to their origin. Record negative findings narrowly.



## Highest-priority editorial rule: genuinely global, non-Anglo discovery

This is the project's highest research priority and overrides convenience, English-language visibility and the inherited archive queue. Research must be genuinely global, with sustained attention to Africa, Asia, Latin America and the Caribbean, the Pacific, the Middle East, and eastern/southeastern Europe. Start from local languages, local terminology, community memory and regional sources; do not let North American or western European material dominate simply because it is easier to index. Preserve original scripts, titles, names and cultural context. English-language visibility is never a proxy for musical importance. Rotate regions deliberately and record which regions/languages remain under-researched.

The small/smol web is a core discovery territory, not nostalgia or a supplementary resource. Search old personal domains, surviving private homepages, GeoCities/Angelfire-era sites and their mirrors, Blogspot/Blogger, WordPress, LiveJournal, Tumblr, independent forums, zines, webrings, blogrolls, guestbooks, link directories, cassette blogs, micro-label pages, regional collector sites and local-language hobby pages. Use Wiby, Marginalia, Weirdnet, Old'aVista, Neocities/Nekoweb directories, smallweb.cc and web archives/Wayback snapshots; follow outbound links, archived link lists, page source references and filenames. A site that is abandoned, absent from mainstream search or only visible in a web archive is still a potential first-hand source. Treat it as a lead, then verify claims against primary objects, testimony or independent records. Do not assume every old private site is still online; distinguish live pages, archived snapshots and dead links.

## 6. Global research method

Build coverage by region, language, decade, recording medium and discovery pathway. At minimum, maintain a country/territory search matrix with local-language search terms, archives searched, relevant labels/scenes, leads and gaps. Search beyond English and major commercial platforms.

Potential discovery routes include:
- national libraries, sound archives, radio archives, museums, university special collections and local history collections;
- psychiatric hospital archives, institutional magazines, patient newspapers, annual reports, programmes and music therapy collections;
- family papers, personal cassette collections, estate archives and oral histories;
- independent labels, cassette networks, mail-order catalogues, fanzines, local record shops and community radio;
- outsider-art organisations, disability-led arts organisations, self-taught artist networks and community workshops;
- regional music historians, collectors, archivists, journalists, recordists and local musicians;
- digitised collections, physical catalogues, record-company files, matrix books, tape lists and private press discographies.

Search by artist aliases, local spellings, scripts, institution names (including historic names), local terms for psychiatric settings, recording formats, label names, and phrases used by local communities. Preserve original script and spelling with transliteration. Machine translation and OCR are discovery aids, not final evidence for lyrics or historical claims.

## 7. Metadata for each recording

Capture as many fields as evidence allows:
- stable project ID; status; title as written; alternate titles; original script and transliteration;
- artist, group, alias and credited roles (composer, lyricist, performer, arranger, recorder, producer, compiler); identity confidence;
- pathway tags and concise evidence-based inclusion rationale;
- country/region and relevant places; distinguish artist origin, recording location, institution, label and archive;
- composition, performance, recording/fixation, issue, reissue, acquisition, digitisation and upload dates separately;
- language, musical form/genre (including “unclassified”), instrumentation and listening notes;
- medium, label/publisher, catalogue number, matrix/take, format, edition, physical description and completeness;
- archive/collection, shelfmark/accession number, catalogue page and direct audio/video/score link;
- provenance and transfer chain; identify original, copy, excerpt, restoration, reissue or derivative;
- source citations for significant claims; rights/access conditions separately;
- ethical/contextual notes, disagreements, unresolved questions and next verification step.

Describe sound only after listening to an identified version. State which version was heard. Do not invent characteristics from a title, cover, genre tag or second-hand description.

## 8. Ethics, language and representation

Treat artists as makers and people, not curiosities, diagnoses or stereotypes. Use the person's own preferred terms where known. Explain obsolete or offensive historical language rather than adopting it as the project's voice.

- Never infer diagnosis, capacity, consent, identity or biography from sound, style, image or archival metadata alone.
- Distinguish diagnosis from hospitalisation, self-description, institutional label, contemporary allegation and retrospective account.
- Do not equate mental illness, disability, neurodivergence, self-taught practice or marginalisation with artistic quality, danger, genius, authenticity or a particular sound.
- Do not romanticise confinement, coercion, poverty, exclusion, trauma or treatment as a source of creativity.
- Consider consent, power imbalance, institutional control, colonial collecting, race, class, gender, disability and whether recording was voluntary.
- Treat medical files, intimate letters, identifiable recordings and sensitive biographical details with care, especially for living people and descendants. Follow archive restrictions and applicable privacy law.
- Include the person's own words and agency where available; record disagreements among institutional accounts, family/community memory and personal testimony.
- Avoid using “freak,” “weirdo,” “mad,” “crazy,” “primitive,” “naïve” or similar terms as the project's own unqualified descriptions. They may be retained in quotations, historical titles, self-chosen names or contextual discussion.

## 9. Rights and access

Public access is not permission to republish. Assess composition/lyrics, performance, master recording, artwork, photographs, privacy/publicity/moral rights and archive conditions separately. Record rights statements, jurisdiction and date. “Public domain,” “streamable,” “downloadable,” “educational use” and “available in an archive” are not interchangeable. Mark **RIGHTS UNRESOLVED** when appropriate; do not redistribute without a defensible basis.

## 10. Collaboration protocol

The project owner/editor sets priorities, scope and final inclusion decisions. ChatGPT and other contributors assist with discovery, source comparison, metadata extraction, chronology, translation/transcription support and drafting.

- Read this method and the latest `coop.log.md` before each session.
- State search boundaries and record queries, languages and archives searched.
- Give source URLs, page numbers, catalogue IDs and explain what each source proves.
- Separate direct observation, source statement and inference.
- Append dated log entries; do not silently overwrite prior findings or erase disagreements.
- AI-generated translations, transcriptions, summaries and identifications remain provisional until checked. Never cite an AI answer as evidence.
- Never claim to have listened, inspected an object, contacted an archive or obtained permission unless actually done.
- Hand-offs should include: question → sources searched → findings → evidence/uncertainty → files changed → next verification step.

## 11. Contextual register: music without surviving audio

Maintain a separate linked register for scores, manuscripts, lyrics, programmes, concert records, institutional reports, oral histories and documented performances where no surviving recording is known. This material is historically important, but it is not a main-discography recording. If an audio recording is later discovered, link the records and update the status with an audit note.


## 3C. Independent music blogs, personal sites and the small web

Use independent music blogs and the small/smol web as **discovery infrastructure**, not as automatic evidence of provenance. Search beyond mainstream music journalism and large streaming platforms: Blogger/Blogspot, WordPress, Tumblr, Neocities, Nekoweb, personal domains, zines, webrings, blogrolls, micro-label pages, cassette blogs, radio show archives, and independent directory/search projects (e.g. Marginalia Search, Weirdnet, Wiby, smallweb.cc, Neocities tag directories).

Useful search patterns combine environment terms with medium and action: “recorded at/in [institution]”, “songs from [camp]”, “prison sessions”, “inside the jail”, “patient choir”, “cassette”, “wire recording”, “field tape”, “smuggled”, “home-recorded”, and local-language equivalents. Search false friends carefully: a band named ASYLUM, DOG PRISON, or POLITICAL ASYLUM is not evidence of music made in an asylum or prison; a post titled “Released From The Hospital” may be an artist/label name or metaphor. A group called Jail Solidarity may be a political punk band, not incarcerated performers.

For every blog lead, capture the author/post date, original item/release identifiers, stated recording location/date, performers, medium, tracklist, whether the post links to primary audio or merely repeats another source, and whether the music has since been reissued. Follow citations outward to artist/family/label/archive records and cross-check independently. Blogs can preserve first-hand testimony, rare scans, tracklists and contact routes that formal catalogues omit; they can also repeat errors, use unstable download links, or circulate unlicensed copies.

Do not use a blog's downloadable rip as permission to reproduce or redistribute a recording. Prefer the artist/label/archive's authorised listening route. Contact rights holders or archivists before reuse. Small-web directories are starting points for finding people and specialist sites, not curated guarantees of historical accuracy.


## Version-control rule: main only

All project collaboration and agent contributions must be committed directly to the repository's `main` branch. Do not create or continue work on feature, task, agent, or temporary branches. Before editing, verify the default branch and current `main` head; commit and push each completed contribution to `main`, then verify the commit and changed files on `main`. If an agent leaves work on another branch, inspect it, preserve useful unique changes on `main` without overwriting newer work, close any superseded pull request, and remove the obsolete branch when repository permissions/tools allow. Never force-push or reset shared history. Report honestly if a branch cannot be deleted through available controls.
